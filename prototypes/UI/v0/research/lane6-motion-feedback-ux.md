## 2. Findings

### 2A. Feedback timing fundamentals

1. **Three response-time limits.** 0.1 s is "about the limit for having the user feel that the system is reacting instantaneously"; 1.0 s keeps the flow of thought uninterrupted; 10 s is the limit for keeping attention. Percent-done indicators for >10 s; for 2–10 s a busy indicator suffices. Nielsen cites Miller 1968 and Card et al. 1991 and says the limits have not changed. [S20]
2. **Model Human Processor constants.** Perceptual processor cycle ≈100 ms (50–200), cognitive ≈70 ms (25–170), motor ≈70 ms (30–100), eye-movement time ≈230 ms (70–700), effective working memory 7 chunks (5–9), pure working memory 3 chunks, decay half-life 7 s. [S42, secondary]
3. **Fixations last 200–600 ms; saccades take 30–120 ms and cover 1–40°; the eye's latency before the next saccade is at least 100–200 ms; during a saccade vision is suppressed; blinks of up to 200 ms can be ignored by the dialogue because the user cannot see the screen anyway.** [S8, S9]
4. **Sub-second response raises productivity more than proportionally** ("productivity increases in more than direct proportion to a decrease in response time"); the IBM paper cites 0.25–0.6 s response times, never "400 ms" (the "Doherty threshold = 400 ms" meme is a later simplification). Longer system response "seems to disrupt the thought processes". [S44]
5. **Gaze feedback must arrive within ≈250 ms of the gaze event** or users stop associating the feedback with the event, because 250 ms is inside the typical fixation duration and the eye may already have moved on. For dwell selection with dwell ≥250 ms longer delays are tolerable; for fast gaze gestures performance already dropped at 100–200 ms delay. [S11, guideline 3]
6. **Dwell selection is a system-timed, not user-timed, act.** "Using dwell time, the user only initiates the action; the system makes the actual selection after a predefined interval. Therefore, general guidelines on feedback … may not be suitable as such. The feedback should be matched with the dwell time." [S1 §7]
7. **The dwell time caps throughput: 1/td.** With td = 1000 ms the ceiling is 12 wpm. [S1 §1.1]
8. **Seow's response classes (instantaneous 0.1–0.2 s, immediate 0.5–1 s, continuous 2–5 s, captive 7–10 s)** — consistent with S20 but cited from memory of the book, not re-verified this session. [S50, weak]

### 2B. Dwell feedback: what the eye-typing experiments show

9. **Experiment 1 (900 ms dwell = 400 ms onset delay + 500 ms shrinking letter), 13 novices.** Click + Visual was fastest (7.55 wpm in block 4 vs 7.14 Speech + Visual, 7.12 Visual Only, 7.00 Speech Only) and preferred by 62%. Error rates were ≈0.5–1% everywhere; Visual Only had the highest KSPC (1.10–1.15). **Gaze behaviour:** with Visual Only users looked over to the typed text once every ~10 characters (RTE 0.110) versus once every ~20 characters with any audio (0.047–0.051). Audio "brings in a sense of finality" and halves the need to verify. After four sessions everyone said 900 ms was too long and "tiring to the eyes". [S1 §4]
10. **Experiment 2 (shrinking vs no shrinking, same 900 ms, 20 novices).** Shrinking: 7.02 vs 6.65 wpm; re-focus events 0.187 vs 0.297 (59% more premature exits without the animation, t = 4.56, p < .001). Shrinking "helped participants maintain their focus on the key"; 50% preferred it, 65% found it helped concentrate, 2 found it disturbing, and "most participants agreed that shrinking might be disturbing and tiring in the long run, even though it helps novices". [S1 §5]
11. **Experiment 3 (450 ms dwell, 15 experienced users).** One-Level Visual (red background on selection) 10.17 wpm and Two-Level Visual (highlight on focus after 150 ms, red on selection) 10.27 wpm beat Speech Only 9.22 wpm. Speech Only produced KSPC 1.28 (≈14% corrected errors) and significantly more double entries because users paused to listen. Two-Level caused confusion between focus and selection (RTE 0.140 vs 0.087 for One-Level); about half found the focus highlight "extra visual noise", 33% said 300 ms between focus and selection was too short to react to, another 33% valued it. One-Level preferred by 47%. **80% judged 450 ms "just right".** A +120 ms penalty for re-selecting the same key (570 ms) was "insufficient to avoid" double entries for many. [S1 §6]
12. **The six Majaranta et al. guidelines (verbatim headings):** 8.1 Use a short non-speech sound to confirm selection. 8.2 Combine speech with visual feedback. 8.3 Use simple, one-level feedback with short dwell times ("there should be no uncertainty of the exact moment when the selection is done"). 8.4 Make sure focus and selection are distinguishable in two-level feedback. 8.5 Use animation to support focus with long dwell times ("It is not natural to fixate for a long time at a static target … animation should show in a direct, continuous fashion the time remaining to selection … it should be subtle"). 8.6 Provide a capability to adjust feedback parameters. [S1 §8]
13. **Feedback placed away from the target is rejected explicitly.** EyeCons (a closing-eye icon elsewhere) "are not convenient in eye typing, since they require the user to concentrate on the EyeCon icon instead of the target letter." The ERICA shrinking rectangle and the shrinking letter both animate *on* the key; the shrinking symbol "draws the attention toward the center of the key" because motion is a pre-attentive feature. [S1 §2]
14. **Rhythm.** When typing fast, "the participant no longer waits for the feedback, but learns to take advantage of the rhythm inherent in the dwell time duration"; spoken letters break rhythm because their durations vary; a click supports it. [S1 §7, 8.1]
15. **Closing circle survives very short dwells.** With an adjustable dwell (150–2000 ms, start 1000 ms, "speed meter" UI), novices went from 6.9 to 19.9 wpm over ten 15-min sessions while dwell fell from 876 to 282 ms (all below 400 ms by session 10; min 180) and error rate fell from 1.28% to 0.36%. The animated closing circle on the key (colour "chosen so that it disturbed as little as possible but was still easy to see") plus a key-down + click on selection: participants "wanted to keep the closing circle even with very short dwell times" (5 tried without; only 2 kept it off). 9 of 10 rated audio as more or equally important as the visual. The **active selection area was larger than the visible key**. A screen-refresh delay when Shift swapped glyph cases "caused disorientation". [S3]
16. **Two-step feedback at 30% then completion.** Mott et al. changed the key border colour after 30% of the dwell and the key background colour plus an audible click at completion (min 100 ms, max 1000 ms, start 600 ms). Users self-selected ≈330–400 ms baselines. Cascading (likely keys faster by 10% steps, unlikely keys slowed to 1000 ms) gave 12.39 vs 10.62 wpm and cut corrected errors from 14.88% to 9.63%, and was rated as having a more pleasant rhythm (15/17). Step sizes were inspired by the just-noticeable difference so the rhythm is not jarred. Five people with ALS reached 9.51 wpm at a 400 ms baseline with no training. [S6]
17. **Exit time after selection is 170–310 ms** and roughly constant above 500 ms dwell; participants called 500 ± 100 ms most convenient, a 100 ms change in dwell "significant inconvenience", 300 ms produced up to 35% errors; the authors keep automatic dwell within 400–700 ms. [S4]
18. **600 ms rated best for generic object selection** (30 participants; 200/400/600/800/1000/1200 ms; 4×3 grid of 4.16° × 4.47° cells). 200 ms caused Midas-touch corrections (11–14%); 1000–1200 ms made "many participants" think "the interface or the eye-tracking system were not working well"; glasses wearers were significantly slower at 200–800 ms. Fitted optima: 575 ms (letters), 625 ms (dots), 650 ms (icons). [S7]
19. **Very short dwell is fine when the action is trivially undoable.** Jacob used 150–250 ms when "selection of a wrong object followed by a selection of the right object causes no adverse effect"; the lag "is hardly detectable to the user". "We found no case where a long dwell time (over ~ second) alone was useful … it … creates the suspicion that the system has crashed." Fixation detection: 100 ms within 0.5°, continuation within 1°, termination after 50 ms outside, blinks ≤200 ms tolerated. Attribute window double-buffered so changes are not noticed unless looked at. [S8]
20. **Eye + 150 ms dwell beat the mouse** (504 vs 932 ms per circle target; 1103 vs 1441 ms when a spoken letter had to be found). An error "bong" was delayed 1250 ms because faster feedback was annoying ("human pinball machine"). The eye's cost is nearly constant with distance. [S9]
21. **Industry dwell choreography (HoloLens).** "Onset delay: when the user starts looking at a target, nothing should immediately happen … We recommend an onset time of 150–250 ms"; then "start dwell feedback"; then continuous feedback "starting out with a bigger circle or sphere that contracts into a smaller version. Showing an indicator for the final state (small circle) helps to communicate … when the dwell will be finished"; finish "if the user kept fixating the target (for another 650–850 ms)". Dwell is recommended "only … as a last fall-back if neither voice input nor hand input is available". [S27]
22. **Head-gaze dwell guidance that transfers:** avoid "gaze as a weapon" (too much feedback induces anxiety; give time to read before anything selects); "Goldilocks speed" (frequent functions faster fill, consequential ones longer; the fill's animation curve can make the same duration feel faster; offer fast/medium/slow override); start radial fills from the button centre and keep the direction consistent; invert the fill for toggling something off; reveal the dwell target on highlight (progressive disclosure); dwell region may be larger than the icon; a short delay before feedback prevents flicker when passing over; toggles must be exited and re-entered to re-trigger; confirmation pattern = highlight main button and reveal its dwell target, reveal the secondary target only on gaze; avoid the "yo-yo" up/down loop between controls and results. [S30]
23. **MRTK dwell profile fields** name the states a real implementation needs: `DwellIntentDelay` (time until the user is judged to intend interaction), `DwellStartDelay` (until DwellStarted fires), `TimeToCompleteDwell` (additional time to select), `TimeToAllowDwellResume` (grace period during which focus may leave and return without resetting), plus a decay option. [S31]
24. **Windows Eye Control** exposes separate dwell times for typing keys versus function keys/word predictions/mouse controls, a Pause button, an optional gaze cursor, and uses "look off-screen" to cancel a mouse action. [S32]
25. **Subtle hover feedback; never attach things to gaze.** HoloLens guidance: feedback on look "should be kept subtle … slowly blending in and out, visual highlights, or … slow motions, such as slightly increasing the target size"; "Avoid attaching something directly to the user's eye-gaze (for example, a slider or cursor)" because of the "fleeing cursor" effect; allow free observation; "Try not to overwhelm the user with immediate pop-out effects or hover sounds"; note "leave before click" (gaze moves on before a slower confirm input lands). Targets ≥2° (thumbnail at arm's length), plan for 2–3° given ≈1.5° accuracy; fall back after a 500–1500 ms loss-of-tracking timeout, not instantly. [S28, S29]
26. **Apple visionOS:** the hover effect is the model for gaze feedback; "revealing content near a button people are looking at can cause them to involuntarily look at the new content instead of the button"; motion in peripheral vision is especially distracting; "the more rounded an item's shape, the easier it is for people to use their eyes to target it" because eyes are drawn to corners; keep ≥16 pt margin around items or ≥60 pt between centres; "aim to keep one or more of the element's primary views unchanged in both states" of a hover animation; hover delay tiers: none (subtle invitation), short (quick interaction), long (extra information). Do not overuse custom hover effects — they "can … cause visual discomfort". [S23]

### 2C. Dwell duration, target size, Midas touch, gaze-hold

27. **Midas touch** (Jacob): "Everywhere you look, another command is activated; you cannot look anywhere without issuing a command." Users "expect to be able to look at an item without having the look 'mean' something." [S8]
28. **Gaze-hold problem** (Penkar): with labels *inside* buttons, 0.2 s dwell averaged 1.63 selections per answer (many false) and 1.0 s averaged 1.02, but small-button + long-dwell was the hardest; with labels placed *outside* the buttons accuracy was 100% at every dwell, yet attempts rose with dwell (1.17 at 0.2 s → 1.39 at 1.0 s): "users find it difficult to look at a button for a long dwell time, even if they intend to." Best: large button (150 px / 8 cm) + short dwell; a central dot anchor is recommended; they also note horizontal jitter exceeds vertical. [S5]
29. **Accuracy varies more than six-fold between users** (90th vs 25th percentile offset 0.93 vs 0.15 cm in x, 1.19 vs 0.20 cm in y). Precision is significantly worse in the **bottom row and the right-most column**. Targets should be **taller than wide**. Target size for 95% of samples: S = 2(offset + 2σ); recommended ≥1.9 × 2.35 cm with the best filter for 75% of users, 3.28 × 3.78 cm unfiltered; for ~all users up to 5.96 × 6.24 cm. Best filter: weighted average with Gaussian/triangular kernel + saccade detection (+ outlier correction if a 2-frame, ≈32 ms delay is acceptable); it improved precision from 0.53 to 0.29 cm but cannot fix offset. Daylight was ≈0.1–0.15 cm better than artificial light. ALS users in their own setups showed even more offset outliers (90th pct 1.32/1.67 cm). The reactive region can be invisible and larger than the drawn element. [S10]
30. **Dwell benchmarks:** 500 ms dwell beat 750 ms in ISO 9241-9 throughput; eye + spacebar reached 3.78 bit/s vs mouse 4.68. [S47] Ware & Mikaelian: performance "profoundly dropped" below 1.5°; eye selection ≈2× faster than conventional devices (via S7/S8). Hansen et al. 2003 found 500 ms problematic for novices (via S1).
31. **Hick's law does not govern saccadic choice.** Reaction time grows with log2(n+1) for learned choice sets, but the law "does not hold" where items must be scanned (random menus) and "saccade responses" show "no relationship, or even faster responses with more elements"; alphabetical lists with a known target can be subdivided (Landauer & Nachbar 1985). [S45, secondary] Fitts's law for gaze is "at least a controversial topic" (Drewes) [S46]; Sibert & Jacob found the eye's cost nearly constant with distance [S9]; Penkar found the size effect larger than Fitts predicts because gaze-hold dominates [S5].

### 2D. Calibration UX

32. **Static-marker calibration is "difficult", "tedious", "dull and tiring for the eye"; "hardly any publication of eye-tracked user studies … does not report calibration problems".** Pursuit calibration uses a moving target and samples only while gaze–target correlation exceeds a threshold (window 80–160 ms, threshold 0.3–0.7). A Tobii 5-point calibration takes 19 s and reaches <1°. Pursuit along the screen border at constant speed reaches <1° for any duration ≥10 s (≤11.6°/s); 5 s (23.3°/s) was 2.15× worse; smooth pursuit is limited to ≈30°/s and too-slow targets fail correlation. Final accuracy was reached at ~66% of the path, i.e. a 10 s run could stop at 7 s. The target should be as small as possible (words gave worse accuracy than dots). It tolerated a 2.6 s off-screen interruption (resume, no restart); a disguised "please wait" screen calibrated 13/14 users in 13.6 s without their awareness; a circular ATM path calibrated all 20 in 7.8 s. [S12]
33. **A game mechanic that keeps gaze fixed at the point before and after sampling doubles accuracy for unsupervised children** (~0.5 cm vs ~1.0 cm for the standard moving-ball routine; recalibrations 0.5 vs 1.0 per 5 points, the standard routine rising to 1.9 by session 6). The mechanic: an attribute of a 40 × 40 px target cycles every 0.43–0.8 s, the user presses a key when the target attribute appears, data is collected during a 1 s delay, and **feedback is shown "at exactly the same location as the object"**. Just making calibration a game (Firefly/Troll) was not enough. Calibration data collection itself took 23 s; verification followed immediately on different points. [S13]
34. **Smooth-pursuit target design that worked for children with ADHD/learning disabilities:** grey disk 1.5° with a 0.2° central dot alternating blue (2 s) and red (500 ms); children call out "red" (a concurrent task "dramatically" improves pursuit); 6.65°/s; 30–60 s; horizontal even path. Adults: 0.47° (45 dots) vs 0.53° (pursuit), n.s.; children: 0.94° vs 1.15° for 23 dots, with more valid points. The dot routine "requires intense and prolonged concentration" that children could not sustain. [S14]
35. **Pursuit vs 9-point in adults:** RMSE 0.838° (27 s) vs 1.388° (23 s); fitting a better model gave >30% improvement; "letting the method search … over a long time does not necessarily help", so use an early model and refine in the background. [S15]
36. **Data quality is better when the participant signals that they are looking at the point** rather than the operator or software deciding; glasses, contact lenses, eye colour, eyelashes and mascara all degrade quality. [S16, abstract]
37. **Practice rules:** calibration points must "cover the outermost bounds of your gaze"; match background colour and brightness to the real stimuli; validate immediately on *different* points and again at the end to estimate slippage; recalibrate when the validation error is too high or after the user touches/moves. [S48] HoloLens calibration fails for people who look away, for some lenses, eye conditions, smudges, sunlight, hair; apps must degrade gracefully and let users dismiss reminders. [S29]
38. **Flatla et al. (Calibration Games, UIST 2011) found gamified calibration more enjoyable with no loss of data quality** (via S12/S13). Local re-calibration on demand "increased the apparent response speed" because a local offset otherwise makes the system feel slow (Jacob). [S8]

### 2E. Motion design systems and the evidence on animation

39. **Carbon:** productive motion (micro-interactions, "efficiency and responsiveness, while remaining subtle and out of the way") vs expressive (reserved for "occasional, important moments"). Tokens: fast-01 70 ms (buttons/toggles), fast-02 110 ms (fades), moderate-01 150 ms, moderate-02 240 ms (expansion, toasts), slow-01 400 ms, slow-02 700 ms (background dimming). Curves: productive standard (0.2, 0, 0.38, 0.9), entrance (0, 0, 0.38, 0.9), exit (0.2, 0, 1, 0.9); expressive standard (0.4, 0.14, 0.3, 1). Micro-interactions "should use ease-out on user input and fall within 90–120 ms". "If users frequently notice it, remove or minimise it." Always provide a static way to convey the same message. [S33]
40. **Material 3 tokens:** durations short1–4 = 50/100/150/200 ms, medium1–4 = 250/300/350/400 ms, long1–4 = 450/500/550/600 ms, extra-long1–4 = 700/800/900/1000 ms; easing standard (0.2, 0, 0, 1), standard-decelerate (0, 0, 0, 1), standard-accelerate (0.3, 0, 1, 1), emphasized-decelerate (0.05, 0.7, 0.1, 1), emphasized-accelerate (0.3, 0, 0.8, 0.15). [S34]
41. **Fluent 2:** functional, natural, consistent, appealing; larger/longer moves get more time; "Top level" navigation between major pages should "use a quick fade rather than sliding large elements, to avoid disorientation"; keep stagger offsets short; "limit motion to the element in focus"; provide a no-motion setting. [S35]
42. **Apple Motion:** "Don't add motion for the sake of adding motion"; make it optional and supplement with haptics/audio; "aim for brevity and precision"; "generally avoid adding motion to UI interactions that occur frequently"; "let people cancel motion"; on visionOS avoid motion at the edges of the field of view, "consider using fades when you need to relocate an object", give a stationary frame of reference, avoid sustained oscillation (~0.2 Hz is especially sensitive). [S24]
43. **Animated transitions improve perception when staged and simple.** Across six transition types animation beat static for object tracking (F ≥ 22, p < .001) and simple staging beat direct interpolation; heavily multi-staged animations increased error and were not preferred. Use slow-in/slow-out, group similar changes (common fate), minimise occlusion, maximise predictability, "make transitions as long as needed, but no longer", ≈1 s for transitions with movement (Robertson et al. 2002), stage ≈1 s each rather than 0.5 s, and use a dissolve when start and end share no structure. Tversky's Congruence and Apprehension principles are quoted. [S17]
44. **Roles of UI animation today** (practitioner interviews, n = 20): keeping users in context, teaching, improving UX, encoding data, supporting visual discourse. [S19, abstract]
45. **Val Head:** animation that moves objects across "a large amount of space" is most likely to trigger vestibular symptoms; parallax, scroll-jacking, direction mismatch and large perceived distance are triggers; "animation that involves only non-moving properties, like opacity, color, and blurs, are unlikely to be problematic"; ease-in on a button response "feels sluggish and can cause confusion"; "animation should never get in the way of completing a task"; "if it's not adding something … leave it out". [S39, S40]
46. **WCAG:** SC 2.3.3 — motion animation triggered by interaction must be disable-able unless essential; "motion animation does not include changes of color, blurring, or opacity which do not change the perceived size, shape, or position"; technique: `prefers-reduced-motion`. SC 2.2.2 — anything moving/blinking that starts automatically and lasts >5 s needs pause/stop/hide. SC 2.5.8 — pointer targets ≥24 × 24 CSS px (44 × 44 in the enhanced criterion), explicitly for tremor, spasticity, quadriplegia. [S36, S37, S38]
47. **Apple Reduce Motion:** reduce automatic and repetitive animation including zooming, scaling and peripheral motion; tighten springs; track gestures directly; avoid z-axis depth animation; "replacing transitions in x-, y-, and z-axes with fades"; avoid animating into/out of blurs; minimise time-boxed elements that auto-dismiss; in Assistive Access "ask for confirmation twice" for hard-to-recover actions; convey state with more than colour; minimum control sizes 44 × 44 pt (iOS), 60 × 60 pt (visionOS); ≈12 pt padding around bezelled controls, ≈24 pt around bezel-less ones. [S25]
48. **Feedback matched to significance:** status shown passively near the item; alerts only for critical, actionable information; confirm only significant completions; warn before unexpected irreversible loss, never for expected loss; show when a command cannot run and why; use colour + text + sound + haptics so feedback survives any single channel being unavailable. [S26]

### 2F. UI/UX fundamentals that bear on the quadrant design

49. **Modes.** Raskin: an interface is modal "with respect to a given gesture when … the current state of the interface is not the user's locus of attention" and "the interface will execute one among several different responses to the gesture, depending on the system's current state" (*Humane Interface*, p. 42). Mode errors arise when the user is unaware of the mode. Quasimodes are "modes that are kept in place only through some constant action on the part of the user" (spring-loaded). Remedies: avoid modes, "clear indications of state" (Norman), place modal controls "where the focus is in the task flow", prefer undo ("Never use a warning when you mean undo", A. Raskin). [S43, secondary]
50. **Spatial predictability.** "Spatial stability allows users to make rapid decisions about items' locations rather than relying on comparatively slow visual search"; stability is often broken by controls being "elided and repositioned". Switching to a new modality causes a performance dip; "calm notification" (transient, click-through, fades after 1 s) kept 100% of users on the expert path with mean distraction rating 2.45/5; one-to-one mapping between novice and expert actions minimised the dip. Grossman et al.: audio feedback and forced use raised hotkey adoption to 66.6% and 72.8%. [S18]
51. **Progressive disclosure** improves learnability, efficiency and error rate; keep frequent needs on the primary level; do not exceed two levels ("users often get lost"); staged (wizard-like) disclosure fails "when the steps are interdependent and users must alternate between them". [S21]
52. **Heuristics most relevant here:** visibility of system status ("always keep users informed about what is going on"); user control and freedom ("clearly marked 'emergency exit'"); error prevention over good error messages; recognition rather than recall; consistency. [S22]
53. **Gestalt grouping that is cheap for the eye:** proximity follows a pure distance law (log-odds linear in relative distance); proximity and similarity add; common region and element connectedness produce faster discrimination; common fate groups things that move/change together; synchrony groups things that change at the same time. [S41]
54. **Undo versus warning:** the Apple and Raskin sources converge: make selection trivially reversible (Jacob's 150–250 ms rule depends on it) and reserve interruptions for irreversible, unexpected loss. [S8, S26, S43]

### 2G. Sound and haptics

55. A short non-speech click on selection is the single most supported feedback element (fastest condition, preferred by 62%, requested unprompted by short-dwell users, halves text-review glances, supports rhythm). [S1]
56. Speech feedback per letter is harmful at short dwells (pauses, double entries, confusable letters like n/m); speech combined with visual feedback can help novices; variable spoken-word length breaks rhythm. [S1]
57. Vibrotactile, auditory and visual feedback performed equally in gaze tasks; audio/haptic rated above visual in two studies; **when animated visual feedback on the key was available, added haptics brought no benefit in eye typing**; use feedback for simple confirmations rather than abstract information; too-frequent feedback annoys; 20–30 ms pulses for gestures, ~100 ms acceptable for dwell; sequential beats simultaneous stimuli. [S11]
58. Error tones need a delay: an immediate "wrong" sound at 150 ms dwell felt like a "pinball machine"; 1250 ms was comfortable in that study. [S9]
59. Sound supports scanning rhythm and anticipation of the moment to act (Brewster et al. 1996, via S1); Apple requires audio cues to be paired with visual/haptic equivalents. [S25, S26]

## 3. Design implications for Clarte

Strength labels: **strong** = replicated or direct experimental evidence in gaze UIs; **moderate** = single study or strong industry guidance; **weak/extrapolated** = reasoned from general HCI evidence, not tested with gaze.

### 3.1 Dwell / preview / confirm choreography (per target, all 8 quadrants)

Timeline for one selection (defaults; every number user-adjustable per guideline 8.6 [S1]):

| Phase | Time from gaze entry | What happens | Where | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Intent delay | 0–150 ms (letters) / 0–250 ms (commands) | Nothing visible. Jitter and glances must not light anything up. | — | strong [S27 onset 150–250; S1 two-level delay 150; S30 flicker delay; S8 100 ms fixation detection] |
| Focus (preview) | at 150/250 ms | One discrete, static cue *on the target*: background tint or border change, no size or position change. Pair with a persistent "preview" state for the blink-to-confirm variant. | inside the target | strong [S1 8.4, S6 30% cue, S28 subtle feedback, S23 hover effect] |
| Progress | 150/250 ms → threshold | A contracting ring or circle centred on the target that shrinks toward a small visible end-state dot; linear or very mild ease-out; the label glyph stays put. Nothing else on screen moves. | centre of the target | strong for "animate on the target, toward its centre, showing time remaining" [S1 8.5, S3, S27]; the contracting-circle form specifically is moderate (no head-to-head study against a filling ring exists in what I read; shrinking vs none is the tested contrast) |
| Resume grace | any time during progress | If gaze leaves and returns within ≈100–150 ms the ring continues instead of resetting; gaps ≤200 ms (blinks) never reset. | — | moderate [S31 TimeToAllowDwellResume; S8 50 ms termination, 200 ms blink tolerance] |
| Cancel | gaze leaves > grace | Ring drains back over ≈150 ms (reversible transition, not a cut) and focus cue clears. Looking away is the universal cancel; no cancel button. | on the target | moderate [S32 look-off-screen cancel; S24 "let people cancel"; reversal timing weak] |
| Commit | threshold | Sharp, unambiguous completion: fill/colour change on the target + ≤100 ms non-speech click; the chosen chip appears in the centre text by cross-fade. One moment, no second-stage ambiguity. | target + text area | strong [S1 8.1, 8.3; S3; S6] |
| Re-select lockout | after commit | Same target needs exit-and-return, or +150 ms, before it can fire again. | — | strong that double entries are a real problem; the exact remedy is moderate [S1 (570 ms was not enough), S30 toggles] |

Default dwell thresholds (intent delay + progress):
- G1–G4 letter groups: **550–650 ms** default, range 300–1000 ms. Rationale: 600 ms best-rated for object selection [S7]; 80% called 450 ms "just right" after experience [S1]; novices self-select ≈400 ms within a session [S6]; 300 ms caused 35% errors [S4]; above ~1 s users think the system crashed [S7, S8]. **strong**
- Autocomplete / Delete-last: **≈600–700 ms**; End / Speak / Respond-commit: **≈800–1000 ms** or a two-step confirm (highlight main button, reveal its dwell target, secondary target revealed only on gaze). Rationale: "Goldilocks speed" by consequence [S30]; separate typing vs function dwell [S32]. **moderate**
- Trivially undoable preview actions (e.g. previewing an autocomplete candidate without committing) may use **150–250 ms** [S8]. **moderate**
- Let the user drag dwell down over sessions and show it as a speed meter rather than milliseconds [S3]. **strong**
- Keep the gaze-reactive region larger than the drawn button (full quadrant, bleeding to the screen edge) and taller than it is wide [S3, S10, S30]. **strong**

Feedback latency budget: visual change ≤100 ms after the gaze event crosses a threshold, hard ceiling 250 ms including camera + model + render [S20, S11]. If the webcam pipeline cannot meet 250 ms, lengthen the dwell rather than shortening the animation, and time the ring from the *estimated* fixation onset (token tagged with duration-to-date, as Jacob did) so pipeline delay does not shift the rhythm [S8]. **strong on the limit; moderate on the remedy**

Gaze cursor: off by default; offer it only as a setting [S28 fleeing cursor, S1 (cursor hidden because drift distracts), S32 optional]. **strong**

### 3.2 Phase transitions (Listen → Compose → Speak) without pulling gaze

- **Change state by opacity and colour in place; never slide, scale or fly content across quadrants.** Opacity/colour changes are not "motion" under WCAG and are the motion-safe class [S36, S39]; peripheral motion involuntarily captures gaze [S23, S24]; Fluent recommends a quick fade, not sliding, for top-level view changes [S35]. **strong**
- **Stage it: first the quadrant the user just selected settles (commit feedback, ≈150 ms), then the rest of the screen cross-fades (≈250–400 ms, standard-decelerate), then any new text fades in.** One thing at a time, but no more than two stages [S17 staging, ≈1 s total budget; S33 moderate-02 240 ms; S34 medium tokens]. **moderate**
- **Keep the Backend Quadrant geometry invariant across phases**; only labels, colours and enabled-state change. Spatial stability lets users select from memory instead of searching [S18]; EyeCons-style relocation of feedback was rejected because it moves attention [S1]. **strong**
- **Persistent phase indicator in a fixed place** (label + colour + icon, never colour alone) so the mode is readable at a glance without any gaze cost [S22 visibility of status; S25 more than colour; S43 clear indication of state]. **strong**
- **Do not animate the centre text while the user is composing in the bottom row**; let coloured chips appear with a cross-fade and let the decoded word replace them with a cross-fade, not a slide or bounce; Jacob double-buffered the side panel precisely so changes were "hardly seen unless the user were looking directly at it" [S8, S17 dissolve when structure is not shared]. **moderate**
- **Disabled quadrants in a phase** stay visible but quiet (lower contrast, no ring on dwell) rather than disappearing; an empty quadrant that suddenly fills is itself peripheral motion [S23]. **moderate**

### 3.3 The Record↔Respond toggle and the ↔ page flip: avoiding mode errors

Both are modes in Raskin's sense: the same quadrant does different things depending on state that is not the user's locus of attention [S43].

- **Prefer no mode where possible.** Record and Respond are naturally sequential; make R1/R2 a single button whose label *is* the next action ("Listen" → "Pause" → "Reply" → "Done") so the current state is always printed on the control itself (recognition over recall; "controls that show their current value") [S22, S43]. **strong**
- **Give the toggle its own distinct completion feedback** (different click pitch/earcon and a colour family) so entering Respond never feels like selecting a letter [S1 8.4; S11 simple confirmations]. **moderate**
- **Two-step confirm for the destructive end of the cycle** ("End"/"Speak"): highlight + revealed dwell target, longer dwell, never a timed auto-dismiss [S30 confirmations; S25 confirm twice for hard-to-recover actions]. **moderate**
- **Page flip (↔):** (a) keep the three most frequent functions on page 1 and treat page 2 as a second disclosure level, never a third [S21]; (b) print the page's labels on the three buttons at all times and show a persistent page indicator in the ↔ quadrant itself (arrow direction plus "1/2" or two dots); (c) animate the swap as an in-place cross-fade of labels only (≈200–300 ms), no horizontal slide; (d) consider **spring-loaded return**: after one page-2 function is used, fade back to page 1 automatically after a short interval, so the rare page cannot be left armed by accident (a time-limited quasimode) [S43 spring-loaded modes; S35 top-level fade]. (d) is **weak/extrapolated** and must be tested, because an automatic return is itself a state change the user did not request; (a)–(c) **moderate**.
- **Toggles must be exited and re-entered to re-trigger**, and must have a clearly different on/off rendering [S30]. **moderate**
- **Undo beats warnings:** Delete-last-group and Delete-last-word already give cheap recovery; keep them one dwell away during Compose and make every letter-group selection instantly reversible, which is what allows shorter dwells [S8, S54]. **strong**

### 3.4 Calibration flow: what to keep from the reference video, and why

The reference flow (short intro, nine blue reference circles on a light surface, then head-movement references, then a frozen-model free-flow check) already matches most of the evidence. Properties worth keeping or adding:

1. **Small, high-contrast point at the centre of each reference** (a dot inside the circle); words or large discs reduce accuracy [S12]. **strong**
2. **Motion that leads the eye and signals timing:** a guide that travels between points (Tobii's 5-point uses one), then a contraction/pulse at the point so the user knows when sampling starts and ends [S12, S13, S27]. Keep the travel speed well under 30°/s and avoid sharp corner changes mid-sample [S12]. **strong**
3. **Sample only when attention is confirmed**: either the user signals (blink/key) or a correlation/fixation-stability test gates sampling; data quality was better when the participant, not the system, decided [S16, S12]. **strong**
4. **A tiny concurrent task at the point** (e.g. the dot changes colour and the system waits for a fixation-stable moment after the change; feedback appears at the same location) keeps gaze pinned before and after sampling; this doubled accuracy for unsupervised children and worked for ADHD/learning-disability groups [S13, S14]. **moderate**
5. **Cover the extremes of the 2 × 4 grid**, especially the bottom row and right column, where precision is worst [S10, S48]; a border path or corner points plus centre [S12]. **strong**
6. **Validate on different points immediately, show per-point error, and offer a one-point local re-calibration** rather than a full restart; the free-flow check in the prototype already does the first part [S48, S8, S13]. **strong**
7. **Tolerate interruption and resume** instead of restarting (users of this product will look at the person they are talking to) [S12]. **moderate**
8. **Keep the whole thing ≈20–30 s** of actual sampling: 19 s for 5 points (Tobii), 23 s for 5 points + verification in the game, ≥10 s for pursuit; longer routines lose attention [S12, S13, S14]. **strong**
9. **Offer a smooth-pursuit alternative** (horizontal even path, ≈6–7°/s, 30–40 s) for people who cannot hold fixation on a static dot, which is plausible in a dysarthria/motor-impairment population [S14, S12]. **moderate**
10. **Neutral background matching the app's surface; no decorative peripheral motion** during calibration [S48, S23]. **strong**
11. **Explain failure causes and allow dismissal** (glasses, lenses, light, hair, smudges), and re-calibrate when validation error rises rather than on a timer [S29, S16, S48]. **moderate**
12. **A gentle reward/progress read-out at the end** (points collected, error in plain words) improved motivation without hurting quality [S13, Flatla via S12]. **moderate**

### 3.5 Sound design

- One short (≤100 ms) non-speech **click on every commit**; it is the best-evidenced feedback in gaze typing and halves text-review glances [S1]. **strong**
- **Distinct earcons** for phase change, delete, and errors; delay an error tone by ≈1 s so it does not feel punitive [S9]; keep the vocabulary tiny (simple confirmations, not abstract information) [S11]. **moderate**
- **No per-letter speech.** Spoken feedback only at word/sentence commit, and only alongside visual feedback [S1]. **strong**
- Because the user is in a face-to-face conversation, make audio feedback **routable to an earpiece** and switchable, and never the only channel [S25, S26]. **moderate**
- Haptics add nothing when the on-target animation is visible [S11]; skip unless the display is small or occluded. **moderate**

### 3.6 Reduced-motion and comfort fallbacks

- Under `prefers-reduced-motion`: keep the dwell progress (it is essential functional information under SC 2.3.3) but render it as a **stepped opacity/colour ramp or segment fill that changes no size, shape or position**; replace cross-fades of layout with instant swaps plus a 150 ms opacity fade; remove any scale on press, any stagger, any blur animation [S36, S25, S47]. **strong**
- Nothing on screen may move or blink unprompted for more than 5 s without a way to stop it (e.g. an idle "listening" pulse must be pausable) [S37]. **strong**
- Avoid sustained oscillation (~0.2 Hz) in any ambient indicator [S24]. **moderate**
- Provide the "no motion" setting next to dwell speed, so a person who finds the shrinking ring tiring over long sessions (a stated risk in S1) can switch to a static two-state cue. **strong**

### 3.7 What to avoid

- A visible gaze cursor or anything that follows the eye [S28, S1]. strong
- Feedback, tooltips or previews that appear *next to* the target [S23, S1]. strong
- Sliding, scaling or bouncing transitions between phases or pages; motion in quadrants the user is not looking at [S23, S24, S35, S39]. strong
- Dwell times under ≈300 ms for committing letters, or over ≈1 s for anything frequent [S4, S7, S8]. strong
- Two-level feedback where focus and selection are <300 ms apart or look alike [S1]. strong
- Spoken letter names; speech as the only confirmation [S1]. strong
- Moving or re-flowing the quadrant grid, or re-ordering G1–G4, between phases [S18]. strong
- Timed auto-dismiss of anything the user must read; time-boxed confirmations [S25]. moderate
- Square-cornered targets with labels in the corners (eyes drift to corners); put the label and the ring at the centre, round the shape [S23, S5]. moderate
- Critical small controls at the bottom-right of the screen; if the bottom row must hold G1–G4, make them tall, extend their reactive area to the edge, and place rarely used controls elsewhere [S10]. strong
- Keyframe-only progress animations that cannot reverse when gaze leaves (the ring must drain, not restart) — project `better-ui` convention and S24 "let people cancel". moderate
- Ease-in curves on anything that responds to the user [S40, S33]. moderate
- Relying on colour alone to encode G1–G4 or the phase [S25]. strong

## 4. Open questions

1. **No direct head-to-head of dwell indicator forms.** The literature compares shrinking vs none [S1], closing circle vs none [S3], and 30%-border + fill [S6]; nobody I found compared contracting circle vs filling ring vs radial wipe vs colour ramp for errors and fatigue. Clarte should A/B two forms in the free-flow check.
2. **Webcam tracker error is larger than the IR-tracker numbers above.** All target-size and dwell figures come from IR trackers (SMI, Tobii, ASL). The WebEyeTrack pipeline will need its own accuracy/precision measurement; Feit's S = 2(offset + 2σ) formula and the Clarte "check results" screen can produce the real target sizes.
3. **Latency of the camera → model → render loop.** The 250 ms rule assumes feedback follows the gaze event quickly; if the pipeline alone costs 150–250 ms, the ring must be timed from estimated fixation onset and dwell may need to be longer than 600 ms.
4. **Target population.** People with dysarthria from neurological causes may have nystagmus, saccadic intrusions or spasticity; gaze-hold tolerance [S5] and pursuit ability [S14] are unknown for them. The smooth-pursuit calibration and a longer resume grace may matter more than in the studies above.
5. **Audio in conversation.** The user is talking to another person; whether clicks and earcons are acceptable to both parties, or should route to an earpiece, is a product decision no paper addresses.
6. **Spring-loaded page return** (3.3 d) is extrapolated from Raskin's quasimode idea; it needs a prototype test because an automatic state change can itself cause mode errors.
7. **Sources I could not read** and that may change details: Majaranta's 2009 dissertation chapter on feedback, Nyström et al. 2013 full results (effect sizes per calibration method), Scarr et al. 2013 TOCHI on spatially consistent interfaces, Cockburn et al. 2014 CSUR, Chevalier et al. 2016 full taxonomy, Carter & Luke 2020 calibration recommendations, Loschky & Wolverton 2007 (gaze-contingent update latency threshold).
