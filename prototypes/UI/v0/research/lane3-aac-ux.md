# Lane 3: AAC UX and interface design for people with dysarthria and motor disabilities

Scope: who the users are, how AAC conversation actually works, what gaze-AAC users keep or abandon, which accessibility rules apply to gaze, visual access, and the "playful vs clinical" styling question. Written 2026-11-07 for the Clarte UI design (laptop webcam gaze, Record-partner -> Respond flow, Backend Quadrant rule of max one actionable element per 2x4 cell).

Evidence grades used below: **strong** = replicated or direct primary evidence; **moderate** = single study, small n, or official guideline from an adjacent platform; **weak/extrapolated** = my inference from the sources to Clarte's specific hardware.

Reading depth key for the table: *full* = I read the extracted full text; *sections* = I read the relevant sections of the full text; *abstract* = abstract or publisher page only; *secondary* = read via a citing paper, support article, or summary.

## 2. Findings

### A. Who the users are and what it means for a gaze UI

1. **The population is wide, and many have more than one impairment.** Fager et al. [12] frame the group as "severe speech and physical impairment": ALS, brainstem stroke/locked-in syndrome, Parkinson's, MS, Guillain-Barre, muscular dystrophy, spinal cord injury, TBI, CP, usually with severe dysarthria plus tetra/quadriplegia. Design for the intersection (speech loss + poor hand control + imperfect vision + fatigue), not for a single diagnosis. **strong**
2. **Eye movements are usually the last reliable channel in ALS, but they do degrade.** Linse et al. [15]: eye movements "are often the least fatiguing if not the only remaining volitional movements"; ophthalmoparesis occurs especially with prolonged survival; nystagmus, alternating strabismus, glaucoma, gaze fatigue and difficulty holding the head still can each prevent device use. Aust et al. [42]: longer saccade latencies and more hypometria, worst in incomplete locked-in state. Spataro [16]: of 30 ALS users, 23% had low daily use, mainly because of eye-gaze tiredness and oculomotor dysfunction. **strong**
3. **Cognition is not intact for a large minority.** Ringholz [44]: ~50% of 279 sporadic ALS patients impaired on non-motor tests, 15% met FTD criteria; Irish population study: ~14% dementia at diagnosis, >40% executive impairment without dementia. Linse [15] adds apathy (~40%), limited motivation (~80% of 92), depression 30-60%, language problems in almost 50%. Operational simplicity is a clinical requirement, not polish. **strong**
4. **CP brings vision problems at very high rates.** Systematic review [40]: ocular abnormalities 34-100% across studies; refractive error 52%, strabismus 48%, nystagmus 11%; CVI estimates 16-70% depending on definition. Dyskinetic CP adds involuntary head movement that defeats standard trackers (Tong & Chan [38]); case series found eye gaze "unsuitable due to too many involuntary movements" for some children [19]. **strong**
5. **MS and PD add oculomotor noise.** MS: at least one eye-movement abnormality in 68.1% of 163 patients; INO prevalence ~34% [41]. PD: saccadic hypometria and fragmented pursuit [41], which means the eye lands short of a target and corrects in steps. Dwell logic must tolerate a landing that creeps toward the centre rather than snapping there. **moderate**
6. **Light sensitivity is common after brain injury.** Photophobia in ~50% of TBI patients vs 10% of controls, persisting into the chronic phase [43]; described as common and debilitating in MS. **moderate**
7. **Medication changes pupils.** Baclofen (common in ALS and MS) induces mydriasis, which disturbs IR calibration and pupil mapping (Eyegaze 101 [14]). Relevance to a visible-light webcam is unknown. **weak/extrapolated**
8. **Fatigue is the dominant workload term.** Even typically developing users rate fatigue as the largest workload contributor in gaze usability work [19]; ALS users describe "Tobii is tiring... I'm good for..." limited sessions [6]; a crying user could not use her eye tracker at all [6]. Pasqualotto [18]: eye tracking beat a P300 BCI on usability (SUS 80 vs 71) and workload (NASA-TLX 33.5 vs 49.8) but workload is still substantial. **strong**

### B. How AAC conversation actually works

9. **Rate asymmetry is extreme.** AAC users produce roughly 12-18 wpm; typical speech is 125-185 wpm [4]. Gaze typing is usually under 10 wpm; one field-logged ALS user averaged 8.1 wpm over six months [10]; AACrobat cites ~10 wpm for ALS gaze users and a ceiling near 20 wpm for motor-impaired users [7]. **strong**
10. **Timing drives perceived competence.** Higginbotham et al. 2016 (cited in [4]) found that when device output lags the conversation, partners judge the user as less capable or disengaged. Kane et al. [6]: "pacing of communications was the most common source of challenge"; topics change before the user can respond; group conversation is "most affected"; A3 is "always a step behind"; partners "break out the phone... because of downtime while he's formulating a response". **strong**
11. **Partners cannot see device state, so they guess.** Kane [6]: "Conversational partners often had low awareness of whether the AAC user was composing a response, and thus were unsure whether to wait"; engaging the device "has that interruptive kind of spin to it". Their proposed fix is a status indicator with three states: listening, typing, about to speak. A6 held the floor by quickly speaking an easy word such as "but" or "also". SLPs in [4] recommend "I'm typing" quickfires, "expectant, inviting pauses", and that partners "look at the user and not the device". **strong**
12. **Help from partners erodes autonomy.** AACrobat formative study [7] (8 gaze-AAC users, 6 partners): half the partners read over the shoulder or finish sentences; 4 of 6 frequent users were neutral-to-very-uncomfortable with spouses helping and 5 of 6 with close friends; "It erodes one's confidence over time. It prevents nuanced conversation by cutting it short". Comfort with help and with sharing in-progress text falls with social distance. Design guidelines: (1) preserve or increase autonomy; (2) engage partners in a way that respects autonomy; (3) if sharing in-progress text, the user controls how much; later (4) support non-co-located use and (5) two-way text. Default sharing granularity in AACrobat: family character-by-character, friends and medical word-by-word, coarser for others. **strong** (n small, but consistent with [6], [4])
13. **Utterance delivery matters as much as composition.** Kane [6]: devices read all text at once; punctuation produces no pause or emphasis ("why put a period if it's not going to pause"); users want "a dial to control the speed as it's playing or just pause it so someone can talk back"; volume is insufficient in restaurants and for parenting; TTS mispronounces names and non-dictionary words. Devices for Dignity [23]: users expect a voice of their own gender, often a regional accent, and enough volume "to shout or argue"; "quick to speak" was rated important by most. **strong**
14. **Own voice is identity.** Kane [6]: A6 "I absolutely love that I don't have a generic voice"; others "missed the window on voice banking". Costello's message banking [26] records legacy phrases with the person's prosody and can be "double dipped" into a synthetic voice; proxy recording exists for people too fatigued to record. Linse [15]: patients often found voice output and voice banking unconvincing, which is a quality bar, not a reason to skip it. **moderate**
15. **Repair is frequent and often fails.** Conversation-analytic work finds frequent repair sequences and slower progression in aided talk, and that using the SGD "was not always effective in the full repair of the conversation" (Bloch & Wilkinson 2004, cited in the search results); AAC users also use laughter and body to mark trouble. **moderate**
16. **Context-aware vocabulary works when it supports recognition over recall.** TalkAbout [5] (5 adults with aphasia, Wizard-of-Oz): location- and partner-based adaptation preferred over object recognition and current events; ratings 7-9/9 for liking; implication is to surface relevant words instead of hierarchy navigation. "Let's Talk About X" [8] extends this to ALS gaze users with image recognition of the environment. **moderate**
17. **Rate gains from prediction are not automatic.** Koester & Levine [11]: with prediction users made fewer selections but each took significantly longer; "the cognitive cost of prediction largely outweighed the keystroke savings". Trnka 2008 [11]: ~50% keystroke savings with no significant rate gain, attributed to scanning a large list. SpeakFaster [10]: top-5 phrase options gave two ALS gaze users 29-60% faster entry, but selecting an expansion took 12.7-21.2 s and total overhead was 5.2 s per word for the AAC user; non-AAC users gained keystrokes but not speed, with an 87% rise in inter-keystroke interval attributed to dual-task load. **strong**
18. **AI suggestions threaten authenticity and agency unless the user stays in charge.** Valencia et al. [9] (12 AAC users, LaMDA macros, 4 suggestions shown so they "fit on the screen without requiring the user to scroll" and are "a manageable number to read and evaluate"): users liked effort savings but found output "had the wrong tone or was simply incorrect"; editing "is counterproductive to the goal of reducing keystrokes"; invested effort signals caring to partners; selecting an automated phrase "made others believe the system did all the work"; without privacy and transparency "they would absolutely not use it"; the system "should be flexible so that it does not impose a scheme but learns from and is customized by the user". Follow-up humour work (2025, cited in [4]) found users trade agency for speed only for time-critical quips. **strong**
19. **Operational demands are the design lever nobody pulls.** Light & McNaughton [1]: operational competence covers access technique and navigation; users typically get "2 to 6 hours of teaching" where one expert user needed ~96 hours; "much less attention has been directed towards improving the design of AAC systems... to reduce operational demands, ease learning, and facilitate use", which is "rather ironic since this component... substantially affects performance and it is also the one that is most easily amenable to change". Caron, Light & Drager [49]: fewer programming steps lets partners respond to the child and add vocabulary just in time. **strong**
20. **Why devices get abandoned.** Johnson et al. 2006 SLP survey [25]: not adjusting the system, attitude, lack of training and support, poor fit; Moorcroft [25]: no supportive community; Devices for Dignity [23] (18 interviews, 37 user and 68 professional questionnaires): users rank "efficient" > "reliable" > "suitable" > "adjustable", most features judged "not currently available", devices "unreliable and complex", slowness "caused deep frustration, particularly if the person had previous experience of verbal communication"; initial training rated "Crap... No-one showed me and (X) how to use it". **strong**
21. **Eye-gaze AAC that gets kept.** Ball et al. [13]: 14 of 15 ALS users adopted ERICA as primary AAC; 100% used it face-to-face; 71% dimmed lights or closed shades, 29% changed bulb type; 50% wore glasses; training averaged 2.8 h; one user with oculomotor apraxia succeeded only when the keyboard was replaced by whole-utterance choices. Acceptance of AAC in ALS is 96% once offered [15]; regular users are younger at onset; worse clinical condition predicts higher acceptance. Caligari [17] (n=35 late-stage ALS): large QoL gain and high QUEST satisfaction. Linse [15]: training ~5 h; caregivers need step-by-step instructions; the Midas touch problem and outdoor light are named device problems; recalibration can reach "as many as 50 times a day" in some households and glasses reflect screen and window light (EyeO stakeholder interviews [39]); current trackers are "cumbersome, sensitive to lighting, and needing frequent recalibration" [50]. **strong**
22. **Children with CP.** Karlsson 2018 review [19]: 756 records, two low-level studies eligible; the CP study (n=10) found all children met at least one goal and 55% of goals overall; 2019 home trial (n=5 dyskinetic CP, two systems, six weeks each) improved goal performance; months of practice may be needed before smaller targets are usable. **moderate** (weak evidence base, consistent direction)

### C. Accessibility rules and platform guidance that transfer to gaze

23. **WCAG 2.2 target size** [27]: 2.5.8 (AA) requires 24x24 CSS px or equivalent spacing; 2.5.5 (AAA) uses 44x44; intent names "hand tremors, spasticity, and quadriplegia". These are pointer minimums; gaze needs far more (see 29-31). **strong (as a floor only)**
24. **WCAG 2.2.1 Timing Adjustable** [27]: any time limit must be turn-off-able, adjustable over a range of at least 10x the default, or extendable with a 20 s warning; auto-advancing content counts as a time limit; turning off is preferred over adjusting over extending. Dwell durations, auto-stop of recording, and auto-speak timers all fall under this. **strong**
25. **WCAG 2.5.2 Pointer Cancellation** [27]: completion on up-event with abort/undo, or no down-event action; intent is accidental activation by people with motor impairments; confirmation dialogs and undo are named alternatives. Dwell has no up-event; the analogue is "progress is cancellable by looking away, and committed actions are undoable". **strong (by analogy)**
26. **WCAG 2.3.3 / reduced motion** [27]: interaction-triggered motion must be switchable off; respect prefers-reduced-motion; vestibular users get dizzy or nauseous. Game Accessibility Guidelines [35] add: "Avoid flickering images and repetitive patterns" (basic), "Avoid any sudden unexpected movement or events" (advanced), "Make interactive elements that require accuracy... stationary" (intermediate), "Include a cool-down period (post acceptance delay) of 0.5 seconds between inputs" (advanced), "Ensure no essential information is conveyed by a fixed colour alone" (basic), "Allow the font size to be adjusted". **strong**
27. **Apple visionOS eye input** [28]: "The minimum area that your element needs for eye target is 60 points"; "achieve the minimum target area combining size and spacing"; "use round shapes like circles, pills, and rounded rectangles. Avoid using shapes with sharp edges" because eyes "focus on the outside, decreasing eye precision"; "keep the shapes flat and avoid thick outlines or effects that call attention to the edges"; "center the text and the glyphs in your elements using generous padding"; keep main content in the centre of the field of view, edges "can be tiring for your eyes, so use these areas for content that you don't need all the time"; every interactive element gets a subtle hover effect that "needs to be subtle and work on top of any content"; keep interactive content at one depth; long looks are a signal of intent (tooltips). **strong (official, adjacent platform)**
28. **Microsoft eye-gaze dwell** [29][30]: dwell only "as a last fall-back" when voice or hands are unavailable (not Clarte's situation, so dwell is primary); two-stage feedback: onset delay 150-250 ms with nothing visible, then continuous progress "starting out with a bigger circle or sphere that contracts into a smaller version", finish after a further 650-850 ms; Windows toolkit defaults: enter/exit threshold 50 ms, fixation 350 ms, dwell 400 ms, repeat disabled by default (MaxDwellRepeatCount 0), repeat delay to prevent inadvertent repeats, switch mode disables dwell. Windows Eye Control [31]: profiles per condition (with/without glasses), close curtains, recalibrate after moving the laptop. **strong**
29. **Clinical dwell guidance** [14]: "The ideal dwell time for a user is 0.5 seconds or less... a dwell time close to 1 second or higher can actually frustrate the user and make it appear that the user is unable to have success"; position the screen "directly in front of the user's face at eye level" (too high causes eyelid fatigue); a good calibration means the user "should be able to access all four corners of the screen"; for CVI "changing the colors of the icons to increase contrast" and a "high contrast outline of the intended target" help; nystagmus is workable if involuntary movements occur "fewer than three times per minute"; strabismus is handled by a partial nasal patch and single-eye calibration; cataract and ptosis mean calibrating the better eye. **moderate (clinical practice paper)**
30. **Dwell parameters measured** (Penkar et al. [36], 21 students, IR tracker, headrest, 65 cm): buttons 170x150 px (5x4 cm, about 4.4 x 3.5 deg), 120x100 px, 70x50 px; dwell 0.2 / 0.4 / 1.0 s. Accuracy (mean selections per task): 1.63 at 0.2 s, 1.21 at 0.4 s, 1.02 at 1.0 s (dwell effect p = 4.65e-35; size n.s.). Difficulty (time excluding dwell): large button 1.79 s, medium 1.92 s, small 3.04 s (size effect p = 2e-10); small button + 1.0 s dwell was worst because "it seems to be difficult to hold the gaze in the clickable area for even just one second, unless the clickable area is big enough" (the "gaze-hold" problem). Text inside the button "provided an anchor for the gaze to hold on to". Conclusion: large buttons with short dwell (0.2-0.4 s) when the label is outside or minimal; large buttons with ~1.0 s when content must sit on the button. **strong**
31. **Tracker error in real use is far worse than datasheets.** Velasco et al. [38]: a Tobii X2-30 without chinrest gave 2.46 deg accuracy and 1.91 deg precision against datasheet 0.40/0.26 deg. Dwell targets of about 4 deg were optimal at 25 cm in the 2025 handheld study [37], and perceived size shifts with distance. Tobii XR guidance [32]: give feedback within ~100-200 ms, give each element a natural focus point (centred label) because "without a natural focus point... or if the UI element has strong outlines, the eyes might wander, causing visual flickering and accidental activation", never draw the raw gaze point ("chasing the gaze point"), use an invisible expanded hit region. **strong**
32. **Commercial AAC conventions worth copying** [32][34][33]: 8 dwell levels and a separate keyboard dwell; a "look away before reselecting" option; feedback as a rotating clock or shrinking dot with adjustable colour and size; a Rest cell that disables every other cell; track-status and eye-visibility cells that show what the camera sees; indoor and outdoor calibration profiles; the TD I-Series Partner Window, a rear-facing 480x128 strip that mirrors typed text "to encourage face-to-face communication". Devices for Dignity [23]: partner-readable displays valued "in a noisy environment, for a private conversation, or when the voice is not working". **moderate**

### D. Visual access and reading

33. **Text size.** Granquist/Legge [45]: preferred angular x-height in a survey was 0.21 deg, "close to laboratory estimates of the critical print size", the size below which reading speed collapses; low-vision readers need an acuity reserve of roughly 2-3x their threshold. **moderate**
34. **Colour cues on AAC displays** [47]: background colour helped adults without disabilities (2019) and adults with Down syndrome in an eye-tracking study (2022), but had no or negative effect in young preschoolers; spatial arrangement cues helped consistently; clustering by internal symbol colour sped search. For adults, colour-grouping the four letter groups is defensible as long as position is stable and letters are also printed. **moderate**
35. **Nystagmus and calibration** [46]: standard calibration assumes stable fixation; robust (Procrustes) fitting and foveation-period detection improve accuracy; the practical rule from [14] is under three involuntary movements per minute. **moderate**

### E. Dignity and aesthetics

36. **Devices that look "strange" mark their users.** Shinohara & Wobbrock [20] (20 interviews): "Smaller devices and those that looked like mainstream counterparts attracted the least attention"; self-consciousness rose "when design attracted attention to differences from mainstream counterparts"; P10: "I want it to look nice... just as glamorous as the next guy"; P5: "a device is no use to you if you won't use it... there's some holes between shame and utilitarianism"; proposal of design for social acceptance; "'cool' devices would indicate a person is capable of as much as everyone else". Light & McNaughton [1] quote a parent: a dedicated device "tells the world 'I have a disability.' Kids using an iPad have a device that says, 'I'm cool.'" **strong**
37. **Adults reject child-coded AAC.** Aging Up AAC [22] (12 autistic adults): "Many AAC apps feel like they're made for kids or students, and it feels infantilizing"; 3 disliked bright colours "especially when already overwhelmed"; 4 wanted "a clear layout with fewer options"; all 12 wanted customization; recommendations include adult-first design and symbol libraries that "feel more mature". Global Symbols [24]: for adults AAC "is their public voice", and play- or school-centred symbol sets feel "restrictive or infantilising". Devices for Dignity [23]: concerns about devices "'looking good', not 'old-fashioned'... and most importantly for many users, the colour of their device", while some "did not care about image". Profita [21] challenges the concealment paradigm: people decorate hearing devices with pride when the design permits it. **moderate-strong**
38. **Personality must survive the device.** Kane [6]: "I'm really a funny guy, but this whole speech..."; "I think I have dumbed down my humor for the masses"; users switch voices for comic effect (accents, Darth Vader); the authors propose an "AAC Turing Test" and measuring authenticity, conversational participation, and intelligibility to varied partners. Warmth comes from the user's words and voice, not from the chrome. **strong**

## 3. Design implications for Clarte

Geometry assumptions for the numbers below: 1440x900 logical px on a 13-14 inch MacBook (about 0.20 mm per logical px), viewing distance 60 cm. Then 1 deg of visual angle is about 10.5 mm or about 52 px (44 px at 50 cm, 61 px at 70 cm). One Backend Quadrant is 360x450 px, about 7.2 x 9.0 cm, about 6.9 x 8.6 deg.

### 3.1 Targets and the Backend Quadrant rule

- **Treat the whole quadrant as the hit region, with a centred visual anchor.** Real-use tracker error of ~2.5 deg (commercial IR, no chinrest [31]) means a webcam system needs targets whose half-width exceeds error plus jitter, so at least ~6 deg (~310 px) per side at 60 cm. A full quadrant (6.9 x 8.6 deg) is close to the smallest sensible target, which supports the user's rule. Put the label or glyph at the centre with generous padding (Apple [27], Tobii [31], Penkar [30]); do not draw thick outlines or sharp corners, and make the visible button smaller than the hit region if the design wants air between buttons (Apple "combine size and spacing"). **moderate**
- **Penkar's "large button" (4.4 x 3.5 deg with a headrest and IR tracker) was the easy case; webcam needs bigger.** Never place two actionable items inside one quadrant, even stacked; the frontend may split a quadrant visually but not functionally. **moderate**
- **Keep every actionable element stationary and at a constant position across phases** (GAG "stationary", Wilkinson arrangement cues [34], Light & McNaughton operational demands [19]). The 2-row x 4-column Backend grid being constant is the single best cognitive-load decision in the brief. Phase changes may relabel a quadrant (R1 -> R2 -> End) but should not move functions between quadrants. **strong**
- **Reserve one quadrant-equivalent for Rest/Pause, or make look-away a rest.** Grid 3 Rest cell, Windows Eye Control pause, Tobii "look away before reselecting" [32][34]. In Clarte the natural rest is the centre text area and anywhere off-screen; make sure dwell never accumulates on the text area. **moderate**

### 3.2 Dwell and selection

- **Dwell budget: 150-250 ms silent onset, then visible contracting-ring progress, total 600-800 ms by default** (Microsoft [28] 800-1100 ms; Windows toolkit 750 ms; Eyegaze 101 "0.5 s or less", "1 s or higher can frustrate" [29]; Penkar 0.4 s with large buttons [30]). Expose it as a setting with at least a 10x range (WCAG 2.2.1 [24]), for example 300 ms to 3 s, and a separate value for the letter groups versus the top-row functions (Tobii's separate keyboard dwell [32]). The prototype's current "steady ~2 s then blink, then second blink" is at the slow, high-effort end; the gaze-hold problem [30] says holding gaze still for 1-2 s is hard unless the target is large, so measure this with real users before keeping it. **moderate**
- **Progress must be cancellable by looking away and must not restart silently** (pointer cancellation analogue [25]). After a selection, require the gaze to leave and re-enter before the same target can fire again, and do not auto-repeat (toolkit default MaxDwellRepeatCount 0 [28]). A 0.5 s post-acceptance cool-down is a GAG advanced guideline [26]. **strong**
- **Destructive or irreversible actions (End, Delete word, Speak to a stranger) get either a longer dwell or an undo.** Prefer undo over confirmation dialogs, since a dialog adds a target and a page. "Delete last G" is cheap to undo; "End conversation" is not, so it should be undoable for a few seconds or require a second dwell. **moderate**
- **Never visualise the raw gaze point to the user** [31]; show state on the target (hover tint, contracting ring). Show a tracking-health cue (Grid 3 track status) somewhere non-actionable, and a one-dwell path to recalibrate; recalibration up to 50 times a day happens in real homes [21]. **moderate**
- **Blink as confirm is acceptable but fragile:** ptosis, Bell's-palsy-like weakness, and eyelid fatigue are documented failure points (one ERICA dropout had impaired eyelid control; ptosis defeats some trackers [14][21]). Keep dwell-only as a mode. **moderate**

### 3.3 Type, contrast, theme

- **Minimum sizes at 60 cm:** the critical print size works out to ~21 px font size (x-height 0.21 deg = 2.2 mm, x-height ratio ~0.52), so UI labels should be at least 28-32 px and the composed message at least 48 px so it reads at 1.5-2x CPS for the user and still at ~1x CPS for a partner reading from 1.2 m across a table. Provide a text-size setting up to 200% (WCAG 1.4.4) because low-vision users need a 2-3x acuity reserve [33]. **moderate (derived)**
- **Contrast:** meet 7:1 for the message and letters (WCAG AAA) and 3:1 for every control boundary and the dwell ring; Eyegaze 101 [29] specifically asks for high-contrast icon colours and a high-contrast outline on the intended target for CVI users. Letters must appear on the G buttons; colour alone cannot carry group identity [26]. **strong**
- **Light and dark:** offer both. Photophobia affects about half of TBI patients [6] and is common in MS, so a low-luminance theme is a medical accommodation, not a style. Caution for webcam gaze: the screen is the main face illuminant in a dim room, so a dark theme may reduce tracking quality; measure it in the Clarte tracker before choosing a default. Ball's finding that 71% of IR users dimmed lights does not transfer to visible-light webcams. **weak/extrapolated**
- **Motion:** keep interaction feedback instant and small; no parallax, no pulsing, no auto-scrolling text; respect prefers-reduced-motion with an in-app override [26]. The calibration animation the user likes is fine as a once-per-session sequence, but nothing may move inside or near gaze targets during composition because moving edges pull saccades [27][31]. **strong**

### 3.4 Phase structure: Listen, Compose, Speak

- **Three states, visible to the partner from across the table.** Kane's prototype indicator exactly matches Clarte's R1/R2 flow: listening (recording the partner), typing (composing), about to speak [11]. Render the state as a large, unambiguous band or colour field that a partner can read at 1-2 m without a second screen, and announce transitions with a short earcon or spoken cue so a partner who is looking at the user, not the device, still knows. This directly attacks the perceived-incompetence effect of mismatched timing [10]. **strong**
- **Floor-holding in one dwell.** During Compose, offer a single "one moment" utterance (A6's "but/also" trick, the SLPs' "I'm typing" quickfire) [11]. It can live on the second page of feature buttons but is better as the default behaviour of the state indicator (visible "composing" removes the need to say it). **moderate**
- **Record phase must be visibly consensual.** Valencia participants would "absolutely not use" context features without privacy and transparency [18]. Show a plain recording indicator the partner can see, process locally where possible, and let the user discard the recording in one dwell. Recording the partner is the most ethically exposed feature in the product. **strong**
- **No time limits the user does not control.** Recording should not auto-stop on a timer the user cannot change; if there is an idle timeout, make it adjustable 10x or off [24]. **strong**
- **Repair is a first-class action.** Give "say again" (repeat last utterance) and "no / not that" within one dwell from Speak, because repair sequences are frequent and often fail [15]. Under the Backend rule these fit the second page of the top row, or as the post-speak relabel of the AC and D quadrants for a few seconds (relabel in place, do not move). **moderate**

### 3.5 Speaking aloud and the partner view

- **Speak the whole utterance by default, with real pauses at punctuation and a speed control**; users explicitly asked for "a dial to control the speed as it's playing or just pause it" and complained that periods produce no pause [13]. Highlight the word being spoken so a partner leaning in can read along, which doubles as the "dual display" users valued in noise and when the voice fails [32]. **strong**
- **Offer speak-as-you-go as an opt-in per partner circle**, following AACrobat's defaults (family character-level, friends and clinicians word-level, strangers whole-thought) [12]; it trades privacy for timing and must be the user's call. **moderate**
- **Voice:** gender-matched and regional options at minimum [13]; support imported banked or cloned voices [14]; a loud mode for restaurants and groups [13]. Do not ship a single generic voice. **strong**
- **Partner etiquette as content, not chrome.** AACrobat's one-time "communication preference" messages ("Please do not finish my thoughts for me", "Please ask only yes or no questions") [12] can be a user-authored card shown to a new partner once; it must not become permanent explanatory text on the composing screen. **moderate**

### 3.6 What the autocomplete (AC) must and must not do

- **Show one candidate, inline, stationary.** Visual search over lists is where prediction loses its gains [17]; even four options forced 12-21 s selection times for a gaze user [17]. With one AC quadrant, show the single top completion as ghost text continuing the message and mirror it on the AC button; accepting costs one dwell, rejecting costs nothing (keep typing). If a second candidate is ever shown, it must occupy a different quadrant and never shift the first. **strong**
- **Never auto-commit, never auto-speak, never replace typed text.** Suggestions enter the message only on explicit selection (AACrobat's "absolute control over the degree to which they utilize co-constructions") [12][18]. **strong**
- **Prefer completion over generation.** Users rejected LLM phrases for wrong tone, invented facts ("wrong funny"), and for making partners think "the system did all the work" [18]. Complete the user's own letters and words; do not propose whole replies unless the user asked for that mode. **strong**
- **Learn the user's own shortcuts and style; do not impose a scheme** [18]. Context from the Record phase (the partner's last sentence) is exactly the "ongoing dialogue" context users wanted, provided it is visible and consented [18][16]. **moderate**
- **Measure rate, not keystroke savings**, when the backend friend evaluates AC; keystroke savings routinely fail to become speed [17]. **strong**
- **Hide AC when it has nothing good.** An empty or low-confidence AC quadrant should show nothing actionable rather than a weak guess; low-quality predictions add a competing target the eye must evaluate. **moderate**

### 3.7 Cognition, learning, support

- **Two pages of three functions is the ceiling** for a population where 30-50% have executive impairment [3]; put the four letter groups, Record/Respond, Delete and AC on page one and never require a page flip mid-word. Keep labels as words or familiar icons; recognition beats recall [16]. **strong**
- **Build training into the product**: typical provision is "2 to 6 hours" against the ~96 h an expert needed [19], and abandonment follows lack of training and support [20]. A guided first session, a visible tracking-health cue, and in-app recalibration are features, not onboarding fluff. **strong**
- **Reliability is ranked above everything but speed** by users [20]; a UI that hides tracker failure will be abandoned. Show problems plainly. **strong**

### 3.8 Aesthetic direction: playful vs clinical vs adult-calm

- **Evidence favours "mainstream premium, adult, calm" over "bubbly playful".** Adults call child-coded AAC infantilising and dislike bright colours when overwhelmed [37]; users want devices that look like mainstream technology and feel "cool" and "glamorous" rather than medical or toy-like [36]; colour of the device matters to many [37]. The user's own "Neuralink-like, monochrome with purposeful colour" instinct is the better fit; the rounded, flat, centred-glyph geometry that gaze needs (Apple [27]) supplies the softness without cartoon shapes. **moderate-strong**
- **Where colour belongs:** state (listening / composing / speaking), the four letter groups while composing, the decoded word's confirmation, and errors. Everything else neutral. This satisfies both the colour-cue evidence for adults [34] and the "no information by colour alone" rule [26]. **moderate**
- **Where warmth belongs:** in the user's voice, their saved phrases and humour, generous type, and unhurried motion in the calibration moment. Kane's participants wanted their personality back, not a cheerful device [38]. **strong**
- **Avoid clinical coldness that reads as a hospital device** (Devices for Dignity "old fashioned... could look smarter") [37]; the target is a consumer product that happens to be medical-grade, which is also the social-acceptability argument in [36]. **moderate**

## 4. Open questions

1. What is the measured accuracy and precision of Clarte's webcam tracker (WebEyeTrack) with free head movement at 50-70 cm? Every target-size number above assumes ~2.5 deg error; if the webcam is 4-5 deg, even a quadrant is marginal and two-step zoom selection may be needed.
2. Does a dark theme degrade webcam gaze tracking in dim rooms? No source covers visible-light trackers; it needs a bench test before choosing the default theme.
3. Dwell-only vs dwell-plus-blink: no study compares them for dysarthric adults with partial eyelid control; the prototype's 2 s steady window should be benchmarked against 0.6-0.8 s dwell with a larger hit region.
4. Does background colour for the four letter groups help dysarthric adults specifically? Evidence is from adults without disabilities and adults with Down syndrome; a quick A/B in the prototype would settle it.
5. How should the partner see state on a single laptop? Options are a top-edge colour band readable from the back, audio cues, or a phone companion (AACrobat); none has been tested on a laptop form factor.
6. How much context from the Record phase do users want the AC to use, and how is consent shown to the partner? Valencia's participants wanted dialogue context but set privacy as a hard condition.
7. What is the decoding latency of the backend group-to-word model, and does the colour-to-word swap in the centre pull gaze off the buttons mid-word? Motion near targets is a documented precision killer.
8. Are repair actions ("say again", "not that") frequent enough in testing to justify permanent quadrant real estate, or is relabel-after-speak sufficient?
9. Beukelman & Light's participation model and the Apple HIG "Eyes" page were not read directly (search budget and JS rendering); someone should confirm the HIG wording on target size and hover before quoting it in the brief.
