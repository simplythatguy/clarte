# Lane 1: Gaze selection mechanics (literature review for Clarte)

Scope: dwell selection, confirmation strategies, feedback, tracker accuracy and drift, target geometry, fatigue, and performance baselines, read for an 8-quadrant (2 rows x 4 columns) webcam-gaze UI on a laptop.

Method note: 31 papers were downloaded as PDFs and read as extracted full text (saved under `scratchpad/research/pdf` and `scratchpad/research/txt`). Items marked "abstract" or "secondary" could not be fetched in full (paywall, search budget exhausted, or Semantic Scholar rate limit) and are reported only to the extent the abstract or a citing paper states. No numbers below are invented; where a figure comes from a citing paper rather than the original, that is stated.

Units: in Feit et al. [32], at 65 cm viewing distance 1 degree of visual angle is about 1.1 cm on screen. On a typical 14-inch 16:10 laptop (about 30 x 19 cm) one backend quadrant is roughly 7.5 x 9.5 cm (about 7 x 8.5 degrees).

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 The Midas touch problem and the dwell trade-off

1. Jacob coined the Midas touch problem: with a short activation threshold "everywhere you look, another command is activated" [1, 2]. He rejected blinks as the selection signal because they force the user to think about blinking [1]. A fixation lasts 200-600 ms, and his fixation recogniser reports a fixation after 100 ms of samples within 1 degree and ends it after 50 ms outside 1 degree; blinks of up to 200 ms are tolerated inside a fixation [1, 2].
2. Jacob found that a 150-250 ms dwell "gives excellent results" only when a wrong selection is trivially undone by the next selection (for example, showing an info panel). For actions that are hard to undo he used button confirmation, and he "found no case where a long dwell time (over 3/4 second) alone was useful" because people do not naturally fixate that long and it "creates the suspicion that the system has crashed" [1].
3. Sibert and Jacob used a 150 ms dwell and found gaze selection 428 ms faster per trial than the mouse on a 3 x 4 circle grid, but 11% of eye trials versus 3% of mouse trials were lost to equipment problems [3].
4. Ware and Mikaelian (1987) compared button press, dwell (about 0.4 s) and an on-screen select button; gaze was a fast selector "providing that the target size is not too small"; for small targets speed drops and errors rise rapidly [4, abstract; dwell value via 28].
5. Zhang and MacKenzie (ISO 9241-9 task, 75 and 100 px circles, 30 Hz tracker): a 500 ms dwell "seemed just right", 750 ms caused impatience and lower throughput; dwell conditions had 0% error; eye+spacebar throughput was 3.78 bits/s versus mouse 4.68 bits/s; larger targets had about 50% fewer time-out errors [5].
6. Penkar et al. (OzCHI 2012; dwell 0.2 / 0.4 / 1.0 s; buttons 2 x 1.3, 3.5 x 2.5 and 5 x 4 cm): with labels inside the buttons, mean selections per correct answer were 1.63 at 0.2 s, 1.21 at 0.4 s and 1.02 at 1.0 s, so short dwell was inaccurate at every size; with the readable content moved outside the buttons there were zero wrong selections at any dwell or size, and large button + 0.2 s was the easiest and most accurate. The authors conclude that "the content (the labels) should not be on the buttons if the dwell click method is used" [24].
7. Tien and Atkins (menu task, 70 Hz tracker): per-trial accuracy was 0.98 at 370 ms, 0.87 at 220 ms, 0.81 at 177 ms and 0.79 at 147 ms, with trial time falling from 2.97 s to 1.85 s; users "quickly learned to achieve accurate selections in 180 ms, but made errors when selections occurred in 150 ms". One participant reported fatigue from repeatedly glancing to an off-screen menu trigger [27].
8. Nayyar et al. pilot (192 acquisitions): a 200 ms dwell caused more than 50 Midas touches and 300 ms more than 40, so they used 400 ms [46 via 28]. Isomoto et al. reduced average dwell to 86.7 ms using Fitts'-law movement-time prediction but at a 10.0% Midas-touch rate [28]; their 2022 system used 600 ms dwell plus a 0.3 degree dispersion threshold and ML intent prediction (AUC 0.90) [29].
9. Paulus and Remijn (30 participants, Tobii 4C, dwell 200-1200 ms): longer dwell was slower but gave higher success and fewer corrections; 600 ms was rated easiest for letters, dots and icons; 200-800 ms selection was significantly slower for wearers of glasses; a preliminary study rated 250 to about 1000 ms as the useful range [30]. Their follow-up grant reports say older adults (56-75) preferred 800 ms (secondary, grant report).
10. Ranges in deployed systems: COGAIN cites dwell times from 100 to 3000 ms depending on tracker, skill, command type and cost of error, and distinguishes continuous dwell (timer resets on exit), accumulated dwell, and adaptive dwell [44, 52]. MacKenzie summarises typical dwell as 250-1000 ms, fixations 200-600 ms, saccades 30-120 ms [45]. Novice eye-typing studies typically use 450-1000 ms [9, 12].

### 2.2 Adjustable, adaptive and cascading dwell

11. Majaranta et al. 2009 (11 novices, 10 x 15 min sessions, dwell adjustable 150-2000 ms via +/- keys, start 1000 ms, closing-circle progress indicator): entry rate rose from 6.90 to 19.89 wpm while self-chosen dwell fell from 876 to 282 ms and MSD error fell from 1.28% to 0.36%; most of the dwell reduction happened in the first three sessions. Adjustment steps were 160 ms at the long end and 25 ms at the short end [9]. This 19.9 wpm remains the fastest reported dwell-based rate [42].
12. Špakov and Miniotas measured exit time (time from selection until gaze leaves the key) over dwell 300-1100 ms; exit time shortened as dwell dropped from 500 to 300 ms and was flat from 500 to 1100 ms, with large between-user variability, so an automatic dwell adjuster needs per-user calibration [10]. Participants in [9] said they would rather adjust dwell themselves than have it adjusted automatically.
13. Mott et al. cascading dwell (SMI REDn, 17 participants, 8 sessions): each key's dwell is weighted by its next-letter likelihood; keys below 1% likelihood get longer dwell; the minimum cascades down as the user types. Bounds were 100-1000 ms with 600 ms start. Result 12.39 wpm versus 10.62 wpm static (+16.67%), corrected error rate 9.63% versus 14.88%, uncorrected 1.45%; users' baseline dwell fell to about 330 ms by session 8 and nobody raised dwell above 600 ms. Five participants with ALS reached 9.51 wpm with about 400 ms dwell [12].
14. Chen and Shi two-step selection: commands on a side menu use constant 400 ms dwell; after "select", each link's dwell varies with a probabilistic model of where the user will look. Best model cut errors 50% relative to a 100 ms uniform dwell at equal speed and cut response time 60% relative to 300 ms uniform dwell at equal error [26].
15. Hamid and Kristensson's 2024 review lists further adaptive variants: dwell adapted to exit time, to key likelihood, to pupil size, and to entry rate (so the system can adapt when the user tires) [42].

### 2.3 Feedback during dwell

16. Majaranta et al. 2003/2006 (900 ms dwell with 400 ms before focus): visual-only feedback gave the highest error (2.77% vs about 1.2% with a click or speech); "click + visual" gave the fastest entry (7.55 wpm vs 7.00-7.14 for others) and was preferred; speech-only was disliked and letters such as n/m are hard to distinguish by ear [7, 8].
17. A shrinking letter (continuous animation of remaining dwell) raised speed from 6.65 to 7.02 wpm and halved re-focus events per key (0.187 vs 0.297), i.e. it holds the gaze on the key; 65% found it helpful but most thought it could tire over long use [8].
18. With a short 450 ms dwell: 9.89 wpm; 80% rated 450 ms "just right"; two-level feedback (highlight after a 150 ms focus delay, then selection) was confusing at short dwell, and a 120 ms extra dwell was needed to re-select the same key (570 ms) to avoid double entries such as "aa" [8].
19. Majaranta's six guidelines: (1) use a short non-speech sound to confirm selection; (2) combine speech with visual feedback if speech is used; (3) use simple one-level feedback with short dwell times; (4) make focus and selection distinguishable in two-level feedback; (5) use animation to support focus with long dwell times, showing the time remaining continuously; (6) let users adjust feedback parameters and dwell time [8]. Mott et al. followed these: key border colours at 30% of its dwell, background colour change plus audible click on selection [12].
20. Windows Eye Control: a button that is still gazed at after activation does not fire again until gaze leaves; looking off-screen cancels an operation; dwell is split into "typing dwell time" and "general dwell time" (function keys, predictions, mouse controls); a Pause button suppresses all dwell [48]. Tobii Windows Control exposes 8 dwell levels; Tobii's iPad guide uses 1.5 s as an example dwell [49]; iOS 18 Dwell Control reportedly defaults to 1.5 s (unverified) [50].

### 2.4 Two-step and confirm strategies

21. Actigaze (UIST 2015, 25 participants): dwelling near a target colours nearby clickables and lights matching confirm buttons at fixed positions along the screen edge; the user then dwells on the confirm button of the same colour. Fixed positions and fixed colours enable procedural memory. Dwell thresholds were 80 ms (highlight) and 200 ms (confirm). Click time improved by about one second versus the earlier "Multiple Confirm" design (which took 7.4 s vs mouse 2.1 s) without loss of accuracy; after subtracting dwell, pure gaze movement cost 1.18-1.39 s; remaining errors were mostly tracker inaccuracy [25].
22. ERICA/zoom and magnification confirm approaches: dwell to magnify then dwell to select; two-step magnification versus three-step: three-step more accurate, similar times; fish-eye lens beat simple dwell (summarised in [25]).
23. Expanding the gaze-responsive area invisibly (Miniotas et al.): a 12 px target with a 120 px response area, plus a "grab-and-hold" algorithm, cut errors from 25.6% to 10.9% (68% for the smallest target) at a 10% movement-time cost; dwell of 750-1250 ms did not change error rate [6].
24. Gaze-to-object mapping (Špakov 2011, nine algorithms, small targets, 500 ms dwell optimal for most): a fractional mapping algorithm (sample counted by its fractional distance to the object centre) gave the highest correct-selection rate and shortest selection time (about 1030  ms vs 1150-1360 ms for the others) but the most wrong selections at long dwell; "competing" and "accurate ending" algorithms were the most resistant to wrong selections. Raw correct rate on small targets was only 46.8%, rising to 65.9% after a per-location offset correction, which shows how much calibration offset dominates small-target failure [39].

### 2.5 Accuracy, precision, drift

25. Feit et al. (80 users, Tobii EyeX and SMI REDn, two lighting conditions, one calibration, nobody excluded): mean accuracy 0.47 cm horizontal / 0.57 cm vertical, mean precision about 0.5 cm; the 25th percentile of fixations was within 0.15-0.2 cm but the 90th percentile was off by 0.93 cm horizontally and 1.19 cm vertically (a more than six-fold spread). Vertical accuracy is worse than horizontal; precision is worst toward the bottom row and the right edge; daylight was 0.1-0.15 cm better than artificial light; quality did not degrade within a session. Their derived targets: at least 1.9 x 2.35 cm with an optimised outlier filter, or 3.28 x 3.78 cm unfiltered, for 75% of users; up to about 6 x 6 cm to cover nearly everyone. Targets should be taller than wide, the responsive region may be invisible and larger than the drawn control, designers should be conservative, and five expert ALS users reported recalibrating 3-10 times a day and still failing several times daily [32].
26. Manufacturer accuracy claims (0.4-0.5 degrees; EyeTribe 0.5-1 degree) are measured with a chinrest; with head yaw, pitch or roll, remote trackers produce large systematic offsets and data loss, worst for EyeLink remote and EyeTribe; offsets as small as 0.5 degrees can change study outcomes; a laptop-bottom mounting position filmed the eyes from lower and gave about twice the spread of a monitor mounting [33].
27. Pupil-size change moves the apparent gaze even when the eye is still: mean 2.6 degrees (range 0.3-5.2), mostly downward and nasal; calibration error was 0.83 degrees on a black background versus 0.39 on white; the authors advise bright, constant backgrounds and calibrating at the luminance that will be used [34]. Choe et al. propose a regression correction of gaze on pupil size [35, secondary].
28. Webcam trackers: WebGazer's online error was 175-210 px, about 4.17 degrees against a Tobii EyeX in the lab, with error growing when users scroll or move; it self-calibrates from clicks, which fails during click-free tasks such as typing [36]. WebEyeTrack reports 2.32 cm on GazeCapture, 4.56 cm on MPIIFaceGaze, needs at most nine calibration samples, suppresses output during blinks, and drifted 20% over a 20-minute typing session (7.24 to 8.72 cm) versus 49% for WebGazer (7.79 to 11.62 cm) [37]. An independent WebGazer measurement found 3.65 degrees with drift over the session [38, secondary]. The 2024 review puts webcam eye-typing rates on par with commercial trackers only for large keys (6.56 vs 6.26 wpm) [42].
29. Real-time filtering: a weighted-average or outlier filter with saccade detection improves precision and can shrink required targets by up to 42%, at one to two frames of delay; it cannot fix accuracy offsets [32]; Kumar et al. add a one-sample look-ahead (about 20 ms) and on-screen "focus points" to anchor the gaze [40, abstract].

### 2.6 Dwell-free, gestures, pursuit

30. Simulated perfect dwell-free typing reached 46 wpm (36 wpm in session one) with a 1.5-key tolerance; the authors argue dwell-based typing has plateaued near 20 wpm [13]. Real dwell-free systems: EyeSwipe 11.7 wpm after 30 minutes versus 9.5 wpm dwell, 14.59 wpm for EyeSwipe 2 [14, 42]; Filteryedping 15.95 wpm able-bodied, 7.60 wpm for users with disabilities [42]. Dwell-free systems cannot pause mid-word, which matters for users with ALS who tire or make eye contact [12].
31. Dasher by gaze: about 25 wpm after an hour versus 15 wpm for an on-screen keyboard, with a fifth of the errors [15]; 17.3 wpm after ten 15-minute sessions [16 via 9].
32. Gaze gestures take 1-2 s each (1900 ms average, 557 ms per stroke), are insensitive to calibration drift, and almost never occur accidentally during web reading except patterns that resemble reading [20]; gestures were "sturdier" than dwell in a game because they depend less on positional accuracy [21]; EyeWrite gestures were slower than a dwell keyboard (about 5 vs 7 wpm) but left fewer errors and were reported as less tiring [17]; pie-menu gestures reached 7.85 wpm, 12.33 expert [18, secondary]. GazeSpeak uses four letter groups chosen by up/down/left/right glances plus word prediction; 97.7% of gesture sequences match four or fewer words from a 5,000-word list; sentence time 80.9 s versus 137.8 s for an e-tran board; per-direction recognition 75-99%, with eyes-closed and down the hardest, and glasses lowering accuracy to 80.4% [31].
33. Smooth-pursuit selection works without calibration and regardless of target size by correlating eye motion with a moving target [22], but pursuit text entry reaches only 2.9-4.7 wpm [42]. Head nods detected from the eye camera reached 93% recognition; settling the gaze on a target took about 330 ms [23].

### 2.7 Fatigue and comfort

34. Dwell adds an active load to the eyes; prolonged fixation and long saccades are named causes of digital eye strain; using gaze only for pointing strains less than pointing plus selection; requiring users to change their blink pattern causes strain and dryness; adaptive dwell showed the lowest fatigue in one study; eye strain is rarely measured, so most reported speeds carry no comfort evidence [41]. Normal blink rate is 10-15 per minute, so blink-click systems need extended blinks and some users find eyebrow raises or blinks tiring [25, 41]. Voluntary-blink thresholds in the literature disagree (200 ms cut-off in [43]; a 2024 study found voluntary blinks shorter). Dwell-free experiments used 10-minute sessions with breaks because performance fell with fatigue [12, 13].

### 2.8 Performance baselines (for expectation setting)

35. Dwell keyboards: 5-10 wpm novices, 19.9 wpm after ten sessions with self-adjusted dwell, 12.4 wpm cascading dwell, 9.5 wpm for users with ALS [9, 12, 42]. Theoretical ceiling with 1000 ms dwell is 12 wpm; with 500 ms dwell and 40 ms saccades about 22 wpm [8, 17]. Group-based gesture spelling with prediction: about 81 s per five-word sentence [31].

## 3. Design implications for Clarte (8-quadrant webcam UI, laptop)

Evidence grades: strong = replicated across several full-text studies; moderate = one or two studies or IR-tracker data extrapolated to webcam; weak = reasoning from mechanism.

- Quadrant size is already in the safe zone for IR trackers (about 7.5 x 9.5 cm vs 3.3 x 3.8 cm needed unfiltered) but webcam error is 2-4 degrees, so treat the whole quadrant as the invisible response region, draw the control smaller inside it, and keep a dead band of about 1 cm either side of quadrant borders where nothing accumulates. Strong for the sizes [32]; moderate for the webcam margin [36, 37].
- Make bottom-row and right-column targets taller and lower-stakes, since precision is worst there; keep the eyes-closed/down gestures out of the design. Strong [31, 32].
- Dwell defaults: 600 ms default, 800 ms for older or glasses-wearing users, user-adjustable from 150 to 2000 ms with coarse steps at the long end and 25-50 ms steps below 500 ms; expect practised users to settle near 300 ms. Strong [9, 12, 30].
- Per-action dwell: short dwell (300-400 ms) for group keys because a wrong group is trivially undone by Delete; longer dwell or a second step for End, Delete-word and mode changes; shorten dwell on the autocomplete key when the model is confident and lengthen keys the model rates below 1%. Strong for the principle [1, 12, 26]; moderate for the specific values.
- Prefer dwell over blink as the primary selector; if blink stays, use it only as an optional confirm with a duration window and never as the only route. Strong [1, 31, 41, 43].
- Confirm pattern for costly actions: fixed-position confirm target that lights up after a short dwell near the action (Actigaze style), not a confirm that appears under the gaze. Moderate [25, 26].
- Feedback: continuous progress animation on the fixated control (ring or shrink), a 150 ms delay before any highlight so pass-through glances show nothing, a short non-speech click at selection, require gaze exit (or add 120 ms) before the same control can fire again, and no speech per letter. Strong [7, 8, 12, 48].
- Keep readable content out of the actionable quadrants: the composed text, predictions to read, and any labels longer than a glyph belong in the non-actionable middle band; the middle band doubles as the resting zone, and looking off-screen cancels. Strong [24, 48].
- Luminance: keep overall screen luminance constant across states, avoid dark-to-bright flashes when a word resolves, calibrate under the same brightness as the working UI, and prefer a light neutral background; a dark theme enlarges the pupil and increases drift on pupil-centre trackers. Strong for IR trackers [34]; weak when extrapolated to appearance-based webcam models.
- Robustness: accumulate gaze over the dwell window with fractional or majority mapping instead of instantaneous hit tests, add hysteresis near borders, stop accumulating when tracking confidence drops, and surface a visible "not tracking" state. Strong [6, 32, 39].
- Drift management: offer one-glance recalibration and schedule a brief check every 10-20 minutes or when the free-flow check shows offset; model head pose. Moderate [32, 33, 37].
- Fatigue: keep frequent targets close together, put rare actions in corners, allow a pause state, plan for sessions of about 10 minutes with breaks, and measure strain in user tests. Moderate [12, 13, 41].
- Avoid: default dwell under 300 ms, identical dwell for every action, auto-repeat on continued dwell, labels that invite reading inside targets, animated content appearing under the gaze, speech-only feedback, critical actions in bottom corners. Strong [1, 8, 24, 32, 48].

## 4. Open questions and conflicts

- Optimal dwell ranges from 150-250 ms [1] through 500 ms [5] to 600 ms [30] and self-chosen 300 ms [9, 12]; the difference is explained by undo cost, tracker noise and practice, so Clarte should expose it rather than fix it.
- Whether pupil-size drift affects CNN-based webcam gaze estimators as it does pupil-centre IR trackers is untested in the sources read.
- Voluntary versus spontaneous blink durations are reported in opposite directions by different studies.
- Dwell-free typing's 46 wpm potential has not been realised in products; whether group-based spelling with a strong language model can approach dwell-free speeds while keeping pauses is open.
- All IR target-size tables [32] need re-measurement with the repository's webcam check results before the 8-quadrant premise is treated as proven.
- Apple and Tobii Dynavox default dwell values were not verified from primary documentation.
