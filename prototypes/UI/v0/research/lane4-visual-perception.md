# Lane 4: Visual perception, colour, typography and legibility for a laptop gaze-controlled communication UI

Scope: evidence for how the Clarte screen should look so that a person with dysarthria or motor disability can read it, find the four letter groups (G1–G4) instantly, and keep the webcam gaze tracker stable. Companion verification script: `lane4-tools/palette_check.py` and `lane4-tools/palette_round2.py` (pure Python; outputs saved next to them). Extracted texts of the PDFs read are in `lane4-pdfs/*.txt`.

Reading method: primary papers were fetched where open (PDF text extracted with pdftotext), otherwise the publisher/PubMed/Europe PMC abstract. "Read" column says which. The web-search budget for the session ran out near the end, so a few planned sources (Stone/Szafir 2014 size–colour model, Wong 2011, Bigelow & Matteson 2011 full text, Ware's book) could not be retrieved; they are marked as such and nothing is cited from memory as if verified.

## 2. Findings

### Colour coding capacity

1. **Preattentive colour search tops out around seven colours.** Healey (S2) found "up to seven isoluminant colors can be displayed simultaneously, while still allowing for the rapid and accurate identification of any one of the seven", with identification time independent of display size, and only when all three criteria (colour distance, linear separation, colour category) are met. Four group colours are comfortably inside this limit.
2. **Absolute identification of hue is ~9 categories; of colour (hue + saturation) 11–15.** Miller (S3) reports Eriksen's 3.1 bits for hue (≈9 categories, my conversion) and Halsey & Chapanis' "about 11 to 15 identifiable colors, or ... about 3.6 bits", with the overall span of absolute judgment "in the neighborhood of seven". Secondary sources attribute a "≤ about 12 hues" rule of thumb to Ware (S43; unverified wording). Four is safely below any of these limits, so the risk is not the count but CVD and low chroma (findings 4–6).
3. **Qualitative palettes rely on hue plus value.** Harrower & Brewer's ColorBrewer qualitative schemes were "built by differences in hue and value" (S8). Crameri et al. (S7) state that "worldwide 0.5% of women and 8% of men are subject to a colour-vision deficiency" and require colour maps to be perceptually uniform, ordered, CVD-friendly and "readable in black and white prints". Okabe & Ito (S4): about 1 in 12 Caucasian males (8%), 1 in 20 Asian males, 1 in 25 African males are red–green colour-blind; rules: "Do not convey information in color only", add shape/position, avoid red–green pairings, pair bright with dark, label directly rather than via a separate key, avoid red text on dark backgrounds.
4. **Simulation result (this lane's script, Machado matrices in linear RGB, CIEDE2000):** every 4-hue palette tested (Okabe–Ito subset, Tol vibrant/bright, two lightness-stepped custom sets) keeps all pairs ≥ 29 ΔE00 apart for normal vision, but the worst pair collapses to ΔE00 ≈ 8–17 under full deuteranopia/protanopia and ≈ 8–14 under full tritanopia. The best observed floors: deutan 28.6 / protan 23.8 / tritan 13.3 (lightness-stepped set E2, dark-theme variant) and deutan 19.4 / protan 12.2–13.2 (E1/E2 light-theme variants); Okabe–Ito core: deutan 16.1 / protan 12.3 / tritan 7.8–11.1. Severity-0.5 anomalous trichromacy keeps ≥ 19 ΔE00 in all sets. Conclusion: colour cannot be the only carrier of G1–G4 identity for the ≈1 in 12 male users with CVD. Position (fixed left-to-right order), the printed range label ("A–F") and a shape or count cue must carry the same information. (Measured on simulated colours; the ΔE00 "comfortable" threshold of ~20 is my heuristic, not a published number.)
5. **Pastel tints destroy distinguishability.** At 70–78 % white (typical "soft highlight" fills on a light theme) the four tints differ by only 5–9 ΔE00 for normal vision and 0.5–3 ΔE00 under simulated dichromacy. At 60 % white: 8–12 normal, 2–4 deutan. Dark-theme tints (55–65 % toward #121212) keep 19–32 normal and 11–20 deutan. So on a light theme the hue must be carried by a saturated element (bar, underline, outline ≥ 3:1 against the page per WCAG 1.4.11, S12), not by a pale fill alone.
6. **Full-strength warm hues fail on white; full-strength blues fail on black.** Orange #E69F00 on white is 2.25:1 (APCA Lc 44); amber #F2B52E 1.84:1; sky blue #56B4E9 2.31:1. On #121212, blue #0072B2 is 3.61:1 (Lc −26), #2B49C2 2.53:1. A single hex set therefore cannot serve both themes; each theme needs its own lightness variant of the same hue (derived values in §3).
7. **Colour variability itself adds clutter.** Rosenholtz et al. (S9) found that their Feature Congestion measure "correlate[s] well with search performance in complex imagery" and that colour variability "does matter for visual clutter" (captured by Feature Congestion but not by edge density or subband entropy). Penacchio et al. (S37): mean local chromaticity difference predicts discomfort ratings (Spearman ρ = 0.62, 95 % CI 0.42–0.76 in Set 1; ρ = 0.43 in Set 2), "large chromaticity differences are relatively unusual in the natural environment", and in Experiment 3 discomfort rose from low to medium chromaticity difference but did not rise further from medium to high. Implication: keep large areas neutral; restrict strong chroma to small, meaningful elements.

### Contrast and legibility

8. **WCAG thresholds and their rationale.** 4.5:1 for text, 3:1 for large text (≥ 18 pt or 14 pt bold) and for non-text UI boundaries, states and focus indicators (S11, S12). The 4.5:1 value is the ANSI 3:1 minimum × 1.5 contrast-sensitivity loss at 20/40 acuity; 7:1 (AAA) corresponds to 20/80 (S11). Ratios are thresholds and are not rounded (2.999:1 fails).
9. **APCA levels (S16).** Lc 90 preferred for body text; Lc 75 minimum for body text (e.g., 18 px/400, 24 px/300); Lc 60 minimum for non-body content text (24 px/400, 18 px/600, 16 px/700); Lc 45 for large/heavy text (36 px/400 or 24 px/700) and fine pictograms; Lc 30 absolute minimum for placeholder/disabled text and large solid icons; Lc 15 the floor for discernible non-text (dividers, focus rings). Reference font Helvetica/Arial x-height ratio 0.52. The documentation states WCAG 2.x "cannot be used for guidance designing 'dark mode'" because it overstates contrast for dark colours; APCA adds "a small boost for very dark colors". Computed for Clarte: #1A1A1A on warm off-white #F5F2EC = 15.6:1 / Lc 97; #F5F5F5 on #121212 = 17.2:1 / Lc −101; #5A5A5A secondary text on #F5F2EC = 6.2:1 / Lc 76; #7A7A7A = 3.8:1 / Lc 62 (good for secondary labels, not body).
10. **Critical print size ≈ 0.2° x-height; fluent range 0.2°–2°.** Legge & Bigelow (S1): "a consensus value for the critical print size for normally sighted readers is 0.2° x-height"; CPS "lies in the range from about 0.15° to 0.3° depending on the individual"; maximum reading speed is held over "a 10-fold range of print size from approximately 0.2° to 2°" (x-heights 1.4 mm to 14 mm at 40 cm). Rules of thumb at 40 cm: visual angle (deg) = point size / 20; 10 pt = 3.5 mm = 0.5°. Mean x-height fraction of 1049 roman typefaces is 0.46 (range 0.28–0.58); x-height correlates 0.94 with mean lowercase letter width, so x-height is the right size unit. Tinker's fastest reading was at 0.21° x-height with a decline at 0.19°. Below CPS, speed falls sharply; above ~2° it declines gradually.
11. **Workplace standard: 20–22 arcmin character height, 22–31 arcmin cap height recommended, > 31 arcmin "should be avoided" for continuous reading (S17, secondary summary of DIN EN ISO 9241-303 and DGUV 215-410).** Formulas: character height (mm) = viewing distance / 172 (20′) or / 155 (22′): 3.5–3.9 mm at 600 mm.
12. **Geometry for a 13-inch 1440 × 900 CSS-px laptop (286.5 mm active width) at 60 cm (computed):** 1 CSS px = 0.199 mm = 1.14 arcmin; 1° = 52.6 px; the screen spans 27.4° × 17.1°; one of the eight backend quadrants spans 6.8° × 8.5°. Font sizes with a 0.52 x-height ratio: 16 px = 0.16° x-height (below CPS; 13′ cap height — too small), 20 px = 0.20° (CPS; no reserve), 24 px = 0.24° / 20′ cap, 28 px = 0.28° / 23′, 32 px = 0.32° / 26′, 40 px = 0.40° / 33′, 48 px = 0.47° / 39′.
13. **Webcam gaze accuracy context (S31, S32):** JavaScript webcam tracking had a mean fixation offset of ~3.9° (172 px of a 1,000-px-wide display) in the lab and ~207 px online (S31); the best appearance-based deep-learning pipeline on home laptops reached 2.4° accuracy and 0.47° precision (S32). With quadrants of 6.8° × 8.5°, an 8-region layout leaves roughly ±1.5–2° of margin inside each region after a 2.4–3.9° error, which is why the quadrant constraint is realistic and why targets must fill their quadrants with generous inactive gutters rather than small buttons.
14. **Stroke width and size (S18):** pixel height, font, stroke width and smoothing all had significant main effects on threshold legibility; bold helped only at the thinnest stroke tested; optimal legibility was reached at 9 px / 10 pt in that 2005 CRT/LCD setup. Humanist sans typefaces cut total glance time by 10.6 % for men versus a square grotesque, with 3.1 % fewer errors for both sexes (S19). Sans-serif, roman, monospaced styles beat serif, italic, proportional for readers with dyslexia; OpenDyslexic did not beat Arial/Helvetica/Verdana (S20; later critique says inter-letter spacing was confounded). No peer-reviewed outcome data for Atkinson Hyperlegible were found (S42); its differentiated glyph design is a reasonable but unproven choice.
15. **Per-word colour highlighting.** No controlled study of word-by-word coloured backgrounds was found. Nearest evidence: background colour of the whole page changes reading time (S21: 341 readers, 89 with dyslexia; Peach #EDD1B0, Orange #EDDD6E and Yellow #F8FD89 gave the shortest reading times; Blue Grey #DBE1F1, Blue #96ADFC and Green #A8F29A the longest, e.g., Peach 12.3 s vs Blue Grey 18.8 s mean for the control group; all texts at 18 pt). A 2026 hover-highlighting study (80 participants) found no reading-speed or comprehension difference between line/sentence/paragraph highlighting and plain text (secondary report). Treat per-word colour as unproven for reading and justified only as a *status* display during composition (finding 7 says it adds clutter, so remove it once the word is decoded).

### Light versus dark mode, and what it does to the tracker

16. **Positive polarity (dark on light) wins across the Ergonomics series.** Proofreading was better with dark-on-light "under darkness and typical office lighting, and with both black-and-white and blue-and-yellow colour schemes"; red-on-green colour contrast "did not compensate for the lack of luminance contrast" (S22). The advantage held for younger and older adults, and the authors "recommend positive polarity for all ages" (S24). It disappears when overall luminance is equated: "Display polarity per se does not affect readability"; the effect is caused by the higher luminance of light displays (S23). Pupils were smaller and proofreading better with positive polarity (S25). Under glance-like reading, white-on-black in a dark room had the worst legibility thresholds, explained by pupil constriction reducing optical aberrations (S26).
17. **Dark mode: mixed, context-dependent.** Sethi & Ziat (S27): negative polarity increased cognitive load (longer search times, larger pupils) for older adults in bright rooms and for younger adults in dim rooms; older adults felt more positive with dark-on-light. Xie et al. (S28): at night, at low screen brightness and low ambient light, dark mode reduced objective fatigue (higher blink rate, more pupil accommodation) while light mode scored lower subjective fatigue and higher preference; preference fell when luminance contrast dropped below 0.725.
18. **Pupil size moves the measured gaze (S29, EyeLink 1000, 39 participants, 60 cm):** changing background from black to white shifted measured gaze by 0.3–5.2°, mean 2.6°, "mostly downward and nasal"; calibration error was 0.83 ± 0.09° on a black background versus 0.39 ± 0.05° on white; peak drift velocity 8.4°/s. The authors advise to "run the experiment in a bright setting, e.g. with white screen background", because pupil constriction "will greatly reduce pupil dynamics". A 2.6° shift is a third of a 6.8°-wide quadrant.
19. **Illumination is the defining difficulty of webcam gaze estimation (S30).** MPIIGaze quantified lighting by mean grey-level of the face and the left–right intensity difference (directional light); cross-dataset error was 13.9° versus 6.3° within-dataset, and the authors state the lighting plots "clearly illustrate the effect of different lighting conditions". No study was found that manipulates UI brightness as a face illuminant; the inference that a light UI helps the webcam in dim rooms is physically plausible but unmeasured (weak/extrapolated).
20. **Halation/astigmatism claim: not substantiated here.** The frequently repeated claim that light text on black "blooms" for astigmatic readers has no primary source in this lane's reading; the APCA author's own note (gist) argues the real problem is insufficient contrast, not polarity, and prefers personal choice. Mark as folklore unless lane finds a source.

### Motion and comfort

21. **Response-time limits (S35):** 0.1 s feels instantaneous (direct manipulation), 1.0 s keeps the train of thought but is noticed, 10 s is the attention limit. **Material 3 tokens (S34):** short1–4 = 50/100/150/200 ms, medium1–4 = 250–400 ms, long1–4 = 450–600 ms, extra-long = 700–1000 ms; easing-standard = cubic-bezier(0.2, 0, 0, 1), emphasized-decelerate = cubic-bezier(0.05, 0.7, 0.1, 1), standard-accelerate = cubic-bezier(0.3, 0, 1, 1). **Apple (S33):** "Don't add motion for the sake of adding motion", "generally avoid adding motion to UI interactions that occur frequently", "Don't make people wait for an animation to complete", keep feedback "brief and precise", avoid peripheral motion and motion of large objects, and avoid sustained oscillation near 0.2 Hz "to which people are very sensitive".
22. **Accessibility rules:** motion triggered by interaction must be disable-able unless essential; opacity and colour changes are not "motion" (S13; prefers-reduced-motion is a sufficient technique). No more than three general or red flashes per second; a flash is a ≥ 10 % relative-luminance swing where the darker state is below 0.80 (S14). Anything that moves, blinks or scrolls automatically for more than 5 s needs pause/stop/hide (S15). Classical critical flicker fusion is 50–90 Hz, but with sharp spatial edges people perceive artefacts above 500 Hz (S36), so avoid strobing or rapidly alternating high-contrast edges (e.g., a fast blinking caret or a rapidly pulsing dwell ring).
23. **Gaze-specific motion hazard (inference from S29 + S13):** any element that moves while the user is dwelling invites smooth pursuit and drags the gaze estimate; large luminance changes (modal dims, white flashes) change pupil size and shift the estimate by degrees. Both are design defects for a gaze UI even when they pass WCAG.

### Hierarchy, grouping, aesthetics

24. **Common region groups without proximity (S10):** elements inside one bounded area are perceived as a group, independent of proximity or closure. One region for the four letter groups and one for the controls makes the 2-row backend grid legible as two "rows" even when buttons span quadrants.
25. **Aesthetics shapes perceived usability (S38, S39, S40):** pre-use and post-use correlations between perceived aesthetics and perceived usability were "strong", and the system's aesthetics, not its actual usability, affected post-use usability ratings (S38); visual appeal is judged within 50 ms and carries into later judgments by a halo effect (S39). In VisAWI (S40), perceived usability after interaction correlated with **Simplicity** and **Craftsmanship** and the total score, "whereas no significant correlations emerged regarding Diversity and Colorfulness"; Simplicity = "clarity, orderliness, homogeneity, grouping, and balance"; subscale α = .85–.89; manipulating only the colour scheme moved only the Colorfulness facet. Across 7 studies, "simplicity (M = 4.72), colors (M = 4.19), and proportion (M = 4.01) were judged to be most important" by users.
26. **Stigma and device appearance (S41, 20 interviews):** "Smaller devices and those that looked like mainstream counterparts attracted the least attention"; devices that "looked unlike anything else" or were "altered versions of mainstream counterparts" made users self-conscious; participants said manufacturers "do not appear to make an effort to make their devices aesthetically appealing"; the authors propose "design for social acceptance" where "cool" devices "would indicate a person is capable of as much as everyone else". For adults using an AAC-like tool in public, this argues against a childish/casino-bright look and for a mainstream, premium, calm appearance with personality carried by shape, type and a small accent.

## 3. Design implications for Clarte (token level)

Each item is tagged **strong** (direct, replicated evidence), **moderate** (one good study or standard, or a near-neighbour task) or **weak/extrapolated** (inference, simulation only, or no study).

### 3.1 Background and theme strategy

- **Default to a light, warm-neutral page, not pure white and not dark.** Positive polarity improves reading for all ages (strong: S22–S26) because it raises luminance and shrinks the pupil; a bright screen also halves calibration error and suppresses pupil-driven gaze drift (moderate: S29 measured on an IR tracker; extrapolated to webcam). Pure white maximises glare for nothing: #F5F2EC (L* 96) with #1A1A1A ink still gives Lc 97 / 15.6:1, above the Lc 90 "preferred" tier (strong arithmetic). Warm rather than cool neutrals are supported by Rello & Bigham's reading-time ranking (moderate; their Peach #EDD1B0 is too saturated for a whole app, so use a desaturated warm neutral and keep Lc ≥ 90 for the message text).
- **Keep mean screen luminance constant across every state.** No dark overlays, no full-screen white flashes, no dark calibration screen followed by a light app, no theme switch mid-session. Each large luminance step can move the measured gaze by ~2.6° on average (strong measurement, S29; moderate as applied to webcam). Calibrate on the same background the app uses. Dwell/selection feedback must change colour or weight, not page brightness.
- **Offer a dark theme only as an explicit night option, at matched perceived contrast (Lc ≥ |90| for the message, |75| for labels), with APCA not WCAG as the gate (S16).** Dark mode's objective fatigue benefit appears only at night at low brightness (moderate: S28) while older adults do worse with it (moderate: S27). Never a mid-grey page: it combines low luminance with low contrast headroom (weak/extrapolated).
- **Neutral means neutral.** Everything that is not a letter-group cue or the one primary action is ink-on-paper greys. Colour variability is clutter that slows search (moderate: S9) and large chromatic contrasts raise discomfort (moderate: S37). This also matches minimal-ui rule 5.

### 3.2 Four group colours (G1–G4): two verified candidate sets

Both sets were checked with Machado 2009 matrices applied in linear RGB (S5, S6), CIEDE2000 pairwise distances, WCAG ratios and APCA Lc (outputs in `lane4-tools/palette_round2_output.txt`). Hues are kept identical across themes; lightness is adjusted per theme so every swatch reaches ≥ 3:1 non-text contrast against its page (S12).

**Palette P2 (recommended): lightness-stepped, protan/deutan-aware.** Order left→right = G1..G4 = dark→light, blue→yellow, so dichromats still see an ordered lightness ramp.

**Rules that apply to either set (strong on the colour-only point, S4, S7, simulation):**
- Colour is never the only cue. Each group key always shows its letter range ("A–F") at label size, sits in a fixed position, and the typed token carries a visible group index or glyph (e.g., 1–4 dots, or the first letter of its range) that survives greyscale.
- Carry the hue in a saturated element ≥ 4 px thick (underline bar, left rule, outline) that meets 3:1 against the page. Do not rely on pastel fills: at ≥ 70 % white the four tints are 0.5–3 ΔE00 apart under CVD (measured).
- If a fill is wanted on the light theme, cap it at ~60 % white (tints such as `#CCCDDC #CCD9D1 #E7D8E9 #E5DBCE` for P2) and keep the bar; ink on these fills is Lc ≥ 77. On the dark theme, 55–65 % mixes toward #121212 (`#3B406F #165E3D #95669B #AD8B39`) keep ≥ 20 ΔE00 normal / ≥ 17 deutan with #F5F5F5 text at Lc ≥ 58–66; use them for the key faces or token fills with the saturated bar on top.
- Never put body text in the group colour on the light theme; only G1 passes Lc 75 (P2 G1 Lc 95, G2 71, G3/G4 57). Keep token text in ink.
- Never use the warm colours as text on white or the dark blue as text on black (finding 6).

### 3.3 Typed tokens and the decoded word (how to colour without harming reading)

- **While composing:** show each selected group as an ink token (the range label or glyph, e.g., "A–F") on a 60 % tint with a 4–6 px group-coloured underline bar; tokens separated by ≥ 0.25 em so the bars read as discrete marks (common region, S10). Tokens are status, not prose, so their colour is justified (moderate).
- **At decode:** replace the token row with the plain ink word in one cross-fade (150–200 ms opacity, no movement), and drop all group colour from decoded words; optionally keep a 1 px neutral underline on the newest word for 1 s so the change is noticed (S13: opacity/colour change is not "motion"; S35: ≤ 0.1–0.2 s feels direct). Persisting per-word colour in the message is unproven for reading (finding 15) and adds clutter (finding 7) (moderate).
- **Ambiguity/alternatives:** show at most one alternative word, in secondary grey (#5A5A5A on #F5F2EC = Lc 76), never a rainbow of candidates (weak/extrapolated from S2/S3 limits and S9).

### 3.4 Type sizes for a 13-inch 1440 × 900 laptop at 60 cm (computed from S1, S17)

| Role | Size (px, x-height ratio ≈ 0.52) | Visual angle | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Message / composed sentence (the major thing) | **40–44 px**, weight 500–600, line-height 1.25 | x-height 0.40–0.44°, cap 33–36′ | Inside the 0.2–2° fluent range with 2× reserve over CPS for reduced acuity; just above DGUV's 31′ ceiling, acceptable for a 1–2 line glance target, not for paragraphs (moderate) |
| Group key labels ("A–F") | **28–32 px**, weight 600 | x-height 0.28–0.32°, cap 23–26′ | Within the 22–31′ recommended band (moderate) |
| Control labels (Record, Respond, Delete, Autocomplete) | **24–28 px**, weight 500–600 | cap 20–23′ | Meets the 20–22′ ISO minimum (moderate) |
| Secondary status text | **20 px minimum** | x-height 0.20° = CPS | Never below: 16 px is 0.16° at 60 cm, under the critical print size (strong on CPS, computed) |
| Autocomplete candidate | 32–36 px, weight 400, grey #5A5A5A | — | Readable but subordinate to the message |

Typeface: a humanist or neo-grotesque sans with x-height ratio ≥ 0.50, open apertures, distinct I/l/1 and a/o, tabular figures; moderate evidence favours humanist shapes at a glance (S19) and sans over serif/italic for dyslexic readers (S20). Atkinson Hyperlegible is defensible on design intent but unproven (S42). Avoid light weights (≤ 300) at any size on tinted fills (S16 size/weight table; S18 stroke-width effect).

### 3.5 Contrast targets

- Message text: APCA Lc ≥ 90 (and ≥ 7:1 WCAG) on both themes. Labels on keys: Lc ≥ 75. Secondary text: Lc ≥ 60. Disabled/placeholder: Lc ≥ 30 and never used for anything the user must read (S16; strong as a standard, moderate as a perceptual claim).
- Every key boundary, state change (preview, armed, confirmed), focus ring and group bar: ≥ 3:1 against adjacent colours (S12; strong).
- Prefer luminance contrast over hue contrast for every state change; hue-only differences such as red↔green at equal luminance do not work (S22, S4; strong).

### 3.6 Motion

- **Frequent interactions (every group selection, every letter preview): no motion; ≤ 150 ms opacity/colour change.** Apple and the better-ui skill agree; keystroke-rate animation is the classic over-animation failure (S33; moderate).
- **Dwell/arming feedback:** in-place, non-moving: a ring or bar that fills by opacity/colour, never a growing or sliding shape; nothing in the periphery moves while a target is armed (S33 peripheral-motion and large-object advice; S29 pupil/pursuit hazard; moderate/extrapolated).
- **Infrequent transitions (phase change Record→Respond, calibration steps):** 250–400 ms (Material medium1–4) with `cubic-bezier(0.2, 0, 0, 1)`; exits 150–200 ms; one orchestrated moment per phase change, matching the Calibration video's unhurried feel (S34, S35; moderate). Under `prefers-reduced-motion`, replace with opacity cross-fades (S13; strong).
- **No idle motion.** No breathing/pulsing logos or rings (0.2 Hz oscillation sensitivity, S33; 5-second rule, S15), no blinking caret faster than 1 Hz, no flashes ≥ 3/s (S14), never a luminance swing of ≥ 10 % over a large area (S14 flash definition; S29 pupil drift).
- **No luminance-changing overlays.** Dimming the page behind a dialog is both a flash-like luminance step and a pupil trigger; use a same-luminance sheet or inline replacement instead (weak/extrapolated from S29).

### 3.7 Hierarchy and grouping

- One bounded region for the four group keys, one for the controls, one quiet neutral centre for the message; regions are bounded by subtle hairlines or background steps of ≤ Lc 15–30, not by coloured frames (S10 common region; S12 3:1 only where a boundary is the sole identifier; moderate).
- Quiet zones: the centre band carries no selectable target so gaze can rest there without consequence (follows from the quadrant rule plus S9 clutter; moderate).
- Keys fill their quadrant with inactive gutters ≥ 1° (≈ 50 px) between regions so a 2.4–3.9° gaze error lands inside the intended region (computed from S31, S32; moderate).
  - *Correction (owner, 2026-11-07):* this describes the **hit region**, not the drawn key. Selection resolves by backend quadrant, so the whole quadrant already catches gaze error however small the drawn key is. The drawn key only has to stay inside its quadrant, one per quadrant; it does not have to fill it. See README rule 1 and BRIEF "Hit region is the quadrant, the drawn tile is smaller."

### 3.8 Aesthetic direction

- The evidence favours the user's "professional, calm, scientific" instinct over the "bubbly casino" direction: perceived usability tracks Simplicity and Craftsmanship, not Colorfulness or Diversity (S40); visual appeal forms in 50 ms and halos later judgments (S39); aesthetics drives perceived usability even when real usability is held constant (S38); adults avoid devices that look "strange" or "medical" and gravitate to mainstream-looking, "cool" ones (S41). Playfulness should come from generous rounded key shapes, confident type and one orchestrated motion, with chroma confined to the four group bars and one accent (moderate overall).
- Light base with graphite ink and a single accent satisfies "monotone like Neuralink" better than a black base for this product, because the tracker and the reader both prefer light (S22–S26, S29).

## 4. Open questions

1. **UI brightness as a webcam illuminant.** No study measured appearance-based webcam gaze accuracy as a function of on-screen luminance in dim rooms. Worth a 20-minute in-house test with the existing prototype: same calibration, two page luminances, measure the "Check results" error.
2. **Per-word colour and reading speed.** No controlled study of word-level coloured tokens. If Clarte keeps colour on decoded words for any reason, run a small A/B on reading time of the message line.
3. **Which CVD type to prioritise.** The lightness-stepped palette (P2) trades normal-vision separation (31–33 vs 38 ΔE00) for a much better deutan floor (19–29 vs 16); given deutan is the most common deficiency this looks right, but the protan floor on the light theme (12 ΔE00) remains thin and relies on the redundant label.
4. **Pupil drift in the webcam model.** Drewes' 2.6° drift was measured on a pupil-centre/corneal-reflection tracker. Appearance-based CNNs may be less or more sensitive; the prototype's "free flow" check could log pupil-size proxies against error to find out.
5. **Older-adult and low-acuity sizing.** The 40–44 px message size assumes ~60 cm and normal-to-moderately reduced acuity; users at 70+ cm or with 20/60 acuity need a user-adjustable size step (one tap, +25 %) rather than a fixed value.
6. **Unretrieved sources.** Stone, Szafir & Setlur (2014) would quantify how much colour difference small marks (bars, dots) need; Ware's exact wording; ISO 9241-303 clause text; a primary source for the astigmatism/halation claim. None changes the recommendations above, but they would firm up the "weak" tags.
