# Lane 2: Ambiguous / reduced keyboards and language-model decoding

Research lane 2 of 6 for the Clarte eye-tracking UI. Scope: prior art and evidence for a 4-group ambiguous keyboard (Clarte's G1–G4 = A–F / G–N / O–S / T–Z) decoded by a language model, with one highest-probability autocomplete slot, plus delete/undo semantics and partner-speech context. Written 2026-11-07.

How to read the evidence labels: **strong** = several independent primary studies converge, or a direct measurement; **moderate** = one solid study or a clear analogy; **weak/extrapolated** = reasoning from adjacent evidence, not tested in a gaze + 4-group setting.

What was actually read: full text of the primary PDF where the table says "full text" (extracted locally with pdftotext from the downloaded PDF); otherwise the abstract or the author/press page. The companion computation (Section 2, Finding 1) is reproducible from `scratchpad/research/scripts/ambiguity4_pure.py`; its raw output is in `scratchpad/research/research_ambiguity_output.txt`.

## 2. Findings

### A. How ambiguous is the 4-group alphabetical split? (own computation + prior art)

**F1. Clarte's exact split (A–F / G–N / O–S / T–Z) is the best of all 2,300 contiguous alphabetical 4-way splits on English unigram frequencies, and an unconstrained optimum gains only ~2 points.** Computation on Peter Norvig's `count_1w.txt` (333k tokens derived from the Google Web Trillion Word Corpus; code on that page is MIT-licensed, data terms not independently verified; web text, so it contains tokens like "nt", "xp", "hp" that inflate collisions slightly). Restricted to the 30,000 most frequent purely alphabetic tokens; word-level, unigram-only (no sentence context):

| Measure (frequency-weighted) | A–F / G–N / O–S / T–Z |
|---|---|
| Target word is the single most frequent word in its code class (top-1) | 77.9% (79.7% on top-10k words) |
| Target within top-2 / top-3 / top-5 | 88.2% / 92.4% / 95.8% |
| Expected extra "next" presses per word if cycling candidates in frequency order | 0.80 |
| Keystrokes per character incl. cycling (space counted) | 1.135 |
| Distinct gesture sequences for 30,000 words | 17,245 |
| Rank among all 2,300 contiguous alphabetical 4-splits (top-10k words) | 1 of 2,300 (median split: 63.9%) |
| Best unconstrained grouping found by local search (heuristic) | 80.2% top-1 (groups imnquwy / befhs / agjkrtvz / cdlopx) |
| Best contiguous split with 3 / 5 / 6 / 8 groups (top-6k words) | 68.7% / 87.6% / 91.1% / 95.0% |

Top-1 accuracy by word length is lowest for 4-letter words (61%: that/they/what, not/now/how, is/no/go…) and above 90% from 7 letters on. The worst collision classes are all function words: by/at, new/may/get, if/he/me, that/they/what, to/us/up, not/now/how, is/no/go, it/my, an/am, as/do. **Strong (measured)**, with the caveat that any real decoder uses sentence context and will do much better (see F3).

**F2. Prior work reaches the same conclusion: alphabetical grouping costs little and buys learnability.** GazeSpeak [1] chose alphabetical clusters explicitly ("learnability was a higher design priority"); its 5,000-word oral-English trie maps to 3,248 unique gesture sequences, "83.5% of sequences match a unique word, 92.6% match two or fewer words, 97.7% match four or fewer words, 99.2% match six or fewer, 99.6% match 8 or fewer." FlexType [2] optimised 4-group sets with a long-span LM and found that letting letters leave alphabetical order gave "only marginal gains in the clarity metrics (1.02%…)", "which we determined would not be worth the additional cognitive" load; its constrained groups were (a–e)(f–m)(n–r)(s–z '). Gong & Tarasewich [11] (as summarised in [2] and an NSF-hosted 2018 paper) found alphabetically constrained ambiguous keypads performed close to unconstrained ones and were easier to learn. Tanaka-Ishii et al. [4] measured conditional entropy of 4-key assignments: alphabetical (abcdef)(ghijkl)(mnopqrs)(tuvwxyz…) = 1.40 bits vs 1.56 and 1.76 for two non-alphabetical alternatives, and wrote that neglecting alphabetical order "will become difficult for the beginners." Lesher et al. [10] showed exact optimisation on the 9-key keypad buys only ~2.5 percentage points over published layouts. **Strong.**

**F3. With a language model, word-level top-1 for a 4-group alphabetical keyboard is ~89–91%, and ≤2 candidates cover ~97%.** FlexType [2] (12-gram character + 4-gram word model in the VelociTap decoder; words entered with the correct tap sequence and context): intended word at n-best position 1 = 89.14% (constrained) / 91.05% (unconstrained); within positions 1–2 = 96.77% / 97.25%; within 1–3 = 98.61% / 98.57%; not in the 6-item n-best = 0.67% / 0.63%. Users cycled only 0.17 swipes per correctly entered word. **Strong.**

**F4. Four groups is roughly the floor at which an ambiguous keyboard still beats a full keyboard in keystrokes; three is not.** Tanaka-Ishii [4]: with PPM adaptation, 4 buttons needed ~5.1 keystrokes/word vs ~5.5 for a full keyboard (≈9% fewer); with 3 buttons it stayed at ~6.0 (worse than baseline); after 5 hours all subjects exceeded 12 wpm, one reached 23 wpm, but the authors admit "a heavier cognitive load" than a full keyboard. My F1 table shows the same shape (3 groups 68.7% → 4 groups 78–80% → 5 groups 87.6% unigram top-1). **Strong.**

### B. Speeds and costs of gaze + reduced input (what to expect)

**F5. Novice gaze users with four directional selections communicate at roughly 3–4 wpm; practice and better selection raise this to 5–12 wpm.** GazeSpeak [1] (20 able-bodied participants in 10 pairs, 5-word/30-character sentences): 137.8 s per sentence with an e-tran board vs 80.9 s with GazeSpeak (interpreter-held phone) and 77.1 s in front-facing mode (p = 0.003 each vs e-tran); one person with ALS did a sentence in 62 s after a brief tutorial. Eye-gesture recognition averaged 86% (up 88.6%, down 75.3%, left 87.8%, right 86.9%, closed eyes 77.5%, centre 98.6%); glasses dropped it to 80.4%. H4-Writer on an eye tracker with dwell [6]: 2.11 wpm vs 3.54 wpm with a mouse (9 novices, 3 blocks), KSPC ≈ 2.6. EyeWrite [29]: ~5 wpm (8 wpm proficient) vs ~7 wpm for a dwell keyboard; theoretical dwell-keyboard limit 22 wpm at 500 ms dwell. Dasher with gaze [28]: 25 wpm after an hour for experts vs 15 wpm on an on-screen keyboard with word completion, with about one fifth the errors. FlexType by touch [2]: 12.0 wpm (constrained) vs 13.5 (unconstrained), n.s.; CER 2.03% vs 1.81%; legally blind users [3] 7.7 wpm, 7.0% CER. **Strong** on the ranges; exact Clarte numbers are unknown.

**F6. Each selection is expensive for AAC users, so savings in actions matter more than screen density.** AAC rates are "often below 10 words per minute" [13, 15]. SpeakFaster [22] measured eye-gaze users' overhead at 5,215 ms per word vs 1,735 ms for non-AAC users, and reviewing five phrase options took "about 3–6 times the average inter-keystroke interval" (FillMask review 2–3×). H4-Writer [5] reached 20.4 wpm by session 10 (165% above session 1's 7.7) only because codes became eyes-free motor reflexes. **Strong.**

### C. Word prediction / candidate lists

**F7. Prediction lists help slow typists only when predictions are good; bad lists train users to ignore them.** Trnka et al. 2008 [13] (28 participants, keystrokes slowed to ~8 wpm with a 1.5 s delay, a list of the top 5 words): advanced trigram prediction was 45.4% faster than basic recency-based prediction and 58.6% faster than no prediction; actual keystroke savings 52.1% (potential 55.6%) vs 19.8% (25.5%) basic; utilisation 93.6% vs 78.2%; copy task 21:36 vs 29:47 vs 32:59. The 2009 TACCESS version [14] reports 8.40 / 5.73 / 5.21 wpm (33 participants, per the secondary summary) and states the basic system "trained users to ignore the prediction list." Koester & Levine [12] found the opposite in the 1990s with slower, keyboard-expert users: fewer selections but each selection took significantly longer, so "the cognitive cost of using word prediction largely overwhelmed the benefit." **Strong** (quality decides sign of the effect).

**F8. More prediction slots do not raise entry rate under dwell selection; keystroke savings flatten at ~6 slots.** Adhikary, Isom & Vertanen 2022 [15] (160 online participants, 1,000 ms mouse-dwell keyboard, 2–9 slots, between-subjects): 9.1, 10.2, 9.7, 10.7, 10.8, 11.0, 10.3, 10.5 wpm for 2…9 slots (F7,152 = 1.15, p = 0.33); keystroke savings 32.0% (2 slots), 45.2% (3), 44.0% (4), 47.5% (5), 51.0% (6), then flat; CER 0.56–2.10%. Authors: "the cognitive cost of checking the additional slots was washing out any gains," but "for some AAC users, each input action can be tiring, so lowering the number of actions required even without an increase in entry rate can be advantageous." **Strong.**

**F9. Looking at predictions too early costs time; the payoff comes after 2–3 letters and for longer words.** Kristensson & Müllners 2021 [16] envelope analysis: net entry rate ranges from −8.8 to +2.9 wpm depending solely on the user's strategy; any strategy that consults predictions before typing a letter slows the user down; "performance gains are only realized if klook ≥ 2"; optimum Lmin = 6, klook = 3. WSTypist [20] (RL model fitted to human data; gaze shift 200 ms, picking a suggestion 760 ms): usage peaks for words up to ~6 characters, words under 5 characters get little use; suggestion accuracy of 60–70% is the best trade-off and a new algorithm is only worth deploying if it exceeds ~80%; recommends showing "the top suggestion directly in the input field." Li & Feit 2025 (cited in [20]): selecting a suggestion can cost up to three times a regular keystroke. **Moderate–strong** (models validated on touch typing, not gaze).

**F10. Always-visible suggestions reduce keystrokes but impair time, yet users prefer them; prediction use correlates slightly negatively with speed in the wild.** Quinn & Zhai [17]: three assertiveness levels (always / never / probability-gated); higher assertiveness cut keyboard actions but "impaired average time performance" and was "subjectively preferred." Palin et al. [18] (37,370 volunteers): word prediction use has a small negative correlation with WPM (r = −0.183) while autocorrect correlates positively (r = 0.237); prediction reduces KSPC (r = −0.232). Arnold et al. [19]: with suggestions people wrote shorter, more predictable text; speed gains shrank for faster typists. **Strong** for touch; relevance to slow gaze users is that the preference and the "less effort" benefit carry over, the time penalty likely shrinks (Trnka's slowed users gained 58.6%).

**F11. In the closest prior art, candidates are shown to the partner, and users wanted more only when the sequence was truly ambiguous.** GazeSpeak [1] shows the most likely word in the input box (its length doubles as the count of gestures so far) plus the top four predictions (scroll for more); the speaker hears the top prediction at word end and does a "look right" gesture to hear the next. Caregivers asked for more predictions when a sequence matched more than four words, especially with custom names; one person with ALS said "Having more options could slow the caregiver down, but the fact that my caregiver and I work independently could mean it will be faster." SpeakFaster [22] shows five options because that matched gaze-tablet screen space and simulation showed diminishing returns beyond five. Valencia et al. [24] showed four generated phrases "so that they fit on the screen without requiring the user to scroll, and to provide a manageable number of suggestions to read and evaluate, comparable to the number of suggestions provided by existing AAC systems." **Moderate.**

**F12. Showing a partial decode inline, with recent text and continuations together, lowers stress.** Ward & MacKay [28] report keyboard users "were unsure whether they had made an error and had to decide between word completion and typing," whereas Dasher "shows recent text and likely continuations together, with no separate completion mode." MacKenzie & Soukoreff [8] describe the two-focus-of-attention cost of attending to both keypad and display, and note Lewis's predictive soft keyboard, whose "uncertain arrangement of the keys … significantly hindered performance" (letters moved around). Tanaka-Ishii [4] displays the raw key-digit string ("41") beside a candidate box that includes longer prefix completions. **Moderate.**

### D. Error handling, delete and undo for ambiguous input

**F13. Working systems separate three repairs: delete last unit, cycle to the next candidate, and replace one word; backspace is avoided where possible.** GazeSpeak [1]: left wink (or both eyes closed ≥ 2 s) "removes the last character from the current sequence"; right wink (or look centre ≥ 2 s) ends the word and speaks the top prediction; right wink again confirms, look right cycles to the next prediction; a further right wink after the last word speaks the sentence. The system "gracefully adds/degrades functionality," still working with no backspace gesture at all. FlexType [2]: one-finger swipe left = backspace one character (group), two-finger swipe left = backspace all characters of the word; swipe up/down iterate forwards/backwards through the n-best list, replacing the current word; sounds mark the end of the list. Users mostly backspaced a whole word immediately when they suspected a mis-tap rather than exploring the n-best. LetterWise [7]: a NEXT key gives the next most likely letter; 50.1% of words need no NEXT, 86% of characters need no adjustment. SpeakFaster [22]: "initial AE failures [can be] repaired without the costly use of delete or backspace keys" by tapping a word chip and either spelling it (KeywordAE) or picking an LLM alternative starting with the same letter (FillMask); FillMask was correct 58.3% (scripted) / 21.4% (unscripted) of the time. **Strong.**

**F14. Probabilistic decoders can absorb input noise instead of forcing explicit correction.** Ticker [31] selects a word only when its posterior exceeds 0.9 and treats false/missed clicks with a learned noise model ("noise compensation allows for a large variety of implicit error correction"); if the wrong word wins the user simply re-enters it. Nomon [30] uses Bayesian updates over click-timing noise, lets "less-precise users [make] extra clicks so that mistakes are unlikely to lead to errors," and exposes an undo option; 16 novices wrote 35% faster than with row-column scanning (5.8 vs 4.3 wpm), an experienced user 9.5 vs 5.7 wpm. Improving FlexType [3] tested decoder parameters that insert, delete or substitute groups to recover from mis-taps. **Moderate** (noise models exist for clicks and taps; none has been published for quadrant-level gaze confusions).

**F15. Dictionary-only disambiguation fails on names; a letter-exact fallback is required.** LetterWise [7]: with only 15% of the least common words missing from T9's dictionary, "T9 is about 30% slower than LetterWise"; LetterWise's prefix model handles non-dictionary words (KSPC 1.15 vs T9 1.007 with all words present vs multitap 2.03). GazeSpeak [1] falls back to a scan-based letter-by-letter mode read aloud per group, and lets the interpreter add names to the trie. Tanaka-Ishii [4] notes the weak point of predictive entry is unknown words and keeps a non-predictive path. SpeakFaster [22] is "English-only and does not tolerate typos." **Strong.**

### E. Context from the conversation partner and LLM-era AAC

**F16. One previous dialogue turn more than doubles abbreviation-expansion accuracy.** Cai et al. 2022 [21] (64B-parameter LLM, word-initial abbreviations): "Including a small amount of context in the form of a single conversation turn more than doubles abbreviation expansion accuracies compared to having no context"; fine-tuned models reach 74–77% top-5 exact match on turn 2 given turn 1; expansion is exact for >70% of phrases up to abbreviation length 10; KSR over all phrases rises from 37.1% (TDC, no context) and 20.0% (DDC, no context) to 49.0% with context. **Strong.**

**F17. In deployment with eye-gaze users, LLM candidates conditioned on dialogue context sped text entry 29–60%, with five options and no backspace-based repair.** SpeakFaster [22]: offline, 57% more motor-action savings than a predictive keyboard; with context about two-thirds of turns were found with a single initials-only call, "roughly halved" without context; LP1 (lab, ALS, eye gaze) 6.54 vs 4.05 wpm scripted (+61.3%, p = 0.010); FP1 (field, 6 months of baseline) 10.4 vs 8.1 wpm (+28.8%, p = 0.0031); users learned the UI in roughly 20–50 phrases. Partner speech was typed in by the experimenter; ASR of the partner is listed as future work with "privacy considerations" as the barrier. **Strong** for the mechanism, **moderate** for transfer to group-decoding.

**F18. Recognising the partner's speech with a commodity ASR (7–16% WER) gives language-model predictions "nearly as good as" perfect transcripts.** Adhikary et al. 2019 [25] (14 participants, 196 two-sided dialogues, three microphone placements): Google ASR WER 7.3% laptop / 7.0% headset / 8.9% conference mic; IBM 10.5% / 10.7% / 16.0%; two-sided dialogue LMs (conditioning on the partner's turns) lowered perplexity (ensemble to 104.3), and "the errors introduced by recognition only slightly increased the perplexity." Wisenburn & Higginbotham 2008 [26] (Converser): partner speech recognition produced stored noun phrases and full utterances; the condition with Converser had a faster communication rate, users reused the noun phrases more than the full utterances, and it "allowed for periodic instances of a fast rate of speech." KWickChat [23]: GPT-2 generation from a bag of keywords plus dialogue history and persona reached ~71% keystroke savings at a WER threshold of 0.65; quality saturates at 4–5 keywords. **Strong.**

**F19. AAC users want generated text to reflect their own voice, to stay editable, and to draw on specific context; they will not trade authenticity for keystrokes.** Valencia et al. 2023 [24] (12 AAC users, four suggestions per macro): phrase suggestions could reduce "time effort and energy and also lessen frustration"; "Turn words into Requests" was rated most useful; users wanted the model to know medical details (with guaranteed privacy), relationship to the partner, location and time of day, and the partner's actual question; they objected to invented details and generic tone; "editing or rewriting an input is counterproductive to the goal of reducing keystrokes"; effortful messages signal caring; "it should be flexible so that it does not impose a scheme but learns from and is customized by the user." Improving FlexType [3] interviewees mostly did not use word suggestions, so the authors left them out of that version. Arnold et al. [19] show suggestions homogenise what people write. **Strong** on attitudes.

**F20. Character-level LLM decoding is fast enough per keystroke, but users should control when predictions apply.** Gaines & Vertanen 2025 [27]: per-character LLM search at ~61 ms per character on GPU (n-gram 0.1 ms), ~10% relative perplexity gain over the AAC n-gram baseline; recommends "giving users control over when predictions are applied, so users can enter unlikely or novel text." **Moderate.**

### F. Coarse-gaze selection designs adjacent to Clarte

**F21. Binary/quaternary gaze selection with off-screen or far-edge targets is a proven, calibration-free pattern.** Look to Speak [32]: 16 phrases, each look left/right halves the set, four movements to speak a phrase, look up to snooze; glances go off the device, not at the screen edge. GazeSpeak [1]: up/down/left/right gestures, calibration in 10 s, one gesture per letter; "looking diagonal was difficult" on e-tran boards. GazeTalk [33]: 12 large buttons because low-resolution tracking cannot reliably hit more, ~6 wpm (secondary). **Moderate–strong.**

**F22. Large targets, instant multimodal feedback and visible letters during learning are consistent across the gaze and 4-key literature.** EyeWrite [29]: accuracy, jitter and the Midas touch "all contribute to the necessity of providing large visual targets"; glancing away clears the stroke. H4-Writer [5]: the on-screen key map "was essential while learning"; becomes eyes-free above ~15 wpm; audio click per key. GazeSpeak [1]: spoken direction after each gesture ("The instant [audio] feedback on which direction I went was also helpful"). Nomon [30]: yellow/white colouring of likely vs unlikely options from the prior. **Strong.**

## 3. Design implications for Clarte

The constraints: eight Backend Quadrants (2 rows × 4 columns), at most one actionable element per quadrant; bottom row = G1–G4; top row = three feature buttons + a page arrow; middle band non-actionable (good: it is where the eyes go to read, which otherwise causes Midas-touch selections).

1. **Keep the alphabetical split exactly as proposed (A–F / G–N / O–S / T–Z).** It is the best contiguous split on English unigrams (F1), the unconstrained optimum gains ~2 points at a large learnability cost (F1, F2), and every comparable system that chose between learnability and clarity chose alphabetical (F2). Print the letter ranges on the keys permanently; do not re-arrange letters dynamically (F12, F22). **Strong.**

2. **Show one actionable autocomplete slot, not a 3-slot list, and make the top decode live inline.** Under a dwell keyboard, extra slots did not raise speed (F8); the consulting cost of a list is 2–6× a keystroke for gaze users (F6, F9); and with a sentence-level LM the top decode is right ~89–91% of the time with ~97% within two (F3). So: the running best decode of the current word sits in the text line (F11, F12); the AC quadrant holds the single best completion/expansion; the second- and third-ranked alternatives may be drawn as small, non-actionable ghost text next to the inline word so the user knows one "other word" action will fix it (F3, F13). **Strong** for one slot; **moderate** for the ghost alternates (untested).

3. **Gate the AC slot by position and confidence.** Mid-word completions should appear only after ≥2 groups have been entered and when the decoder's confidence is high (roughly ≥60–70%); otherwise the slot stays visibly quiet (F7, F9, F10). On unigrams alone the correct whole-word completion is top-1 only ~8–11% of the time after 1–3 groups and ~28–44% after 5–6 (F1 table), so the gating has to come from the context LM, not from the raw groups. At the start of a turn, the same slot should offer a whole-reply suggestion conditioned on the partner's recorded turn (F16, F17) rather than a word. **Strong** (gating), **moderate** (turn-start reply suggestion).

4. **Decode the whole word at word end, commit top-1 automatically, and let the user cycle rather than confirm.** FlexType auto-committed top-1 and let users swipe through the n-best; they needed it for only ~0.17 swipes per word (F3, F13). GazeSpeak required a confirm gesture per word, which costs an action on every word to save one on ~1 in 10 words (F11, F13). **Strong.**

5. **Give D two meanings by state, and add a separate "other word" action.** While a word is in progress, D removes the last group (one glyph); when nothing is in progress (a word was just committed), D removes the last word (FlexType's one-finger vs two-finger backspace, GazeSpeak's character delete) (F13). Cycling to the next candidate must not be D's job; it is a distinct action ("Other word" / "Not that") that replaces the last committed word with the next n-best entry, as in FlexType and GazeSpeak (F13). Consider a long dwell on D for "delete whole word" as a shortcut. **Strong.**

6. **Render the in-progress word as the current best decode, coloured per group, then settle to neutral text on commit.** This matches GazeSpeak's input box (length = gestures so far, content = best guess) and Dasher's "recent text and likely continuations together" (F11, F12), and uses the user's four G colours for exactly one job: telling the user which group each entered glyph came from. Keep the four hues identical on the G keys and in the text. Add an instant per-selection confirmation (short tick plus key highlight) (F22). **Moderate–strong.**

7. **Provide a letter-exact fallback for names and out-of-vocabulary words, and personalise the lexicon.** Dictionary-only decoding becomes ~30% slower when 15% of rare words are missing (F15). A "Spell" feature that turns each G key into a 2-step exact-letter selection (or a GazeSpeak-style read-aloud scan within the group) is required, and new words should be added to the user lexicon with a high prior (F15, F19). **Strong.**

8. **Six top-row features, two pages of three.** Page 1 (composition, arrow points right): **R1/R2 Record → Respond**, **AC accept/complete** (committing the top candidate and inserting the space, so no separate space key is needed), **D delete group/word**. Page 2 (repair and utility, arrow points left): **Other word** (cycle n-best; F3, F13), **Spell** (exact letters/names; F15), **Speak / Phrases** (speak the composed utterance now, and a Look-to-Speak-style halving picker of the user's own stored phrases and the partner-derived noun phrases; F18, F21, F19). Rest/pause should be an off-screen glance (Look to Speak's "look up to snooze") rather than a quadrant (F21). Rationale for the split: the three page-1 actions are used on nearly every word, the page-2 actions on roughly one word in ten or once per utterance (F3, F13). **Moderate** (the set is well grounded; the page assignment is a design judgement).

9. **Feed the Record phase into the decoder as dialogue context, visibly and consentfully.** Run ASR on the partner's turn (commodity WER of 7–16% is fine; F18), show the recognised text in the middle band during the Context Phase so the user can see what the model heard, and condition both the group decoder and the AC slot on it plus the user's own previous turns (F16, F17, F18). Boost the partner's content words and stored noun phrases as candidates (Converser; F18). Keep processing on-device or ephemeral, show a recording indicator, and let the user delete the context (F17, F19). **Strong** for conditioning on the partner turn; **weak/extrapolated** for the specific combination with 4-group decoding, which no study has measured.

10. **Treat gaze group selections as noisy input.** Model the gaze-to-quadrant confusion (especially between vertically adjacent quadrants and at the bottom row, cf. GazeSpeak's 75% recognition for "down" vs 88% for "up") inside the decoder, as Nomon/Ticker do for clicks and Improving FlexType did for taps (F5, F14), so that one mis-selected group does not force a delete. **Moderate.**

11. **Keep generated whole sentences opt-in, short, editable and visibly machine-made.** Users value authenticity and will not spend keystrokes rewriting generic text (F19); suggestions homogenise writing (F10). Limit turn-start suggestions to one slot, prefer completions of the user's own groups over free generation, and let users edit the suggestion by continuing to type groups. **Moderate.**

12. **Expected performance envelope to design and test against.** Novices with four gaze directions: ~3–4 wpm (GazeSpeak's 77–81 s per 5-word sentence); with practice and good decoding, 5–12 wpm is realistic (F5); SpeakFaster's eye-gaze users gained 29–60% from context-aware LLM candidates because their per-action cost is high (F6, F17). Measure actions per word and repair rate, not only wpm (F8). **Strong.**

## 4. Open questions

1. No published study combines gaze-dwell selection of four letter groups with LLM decoding; GazeSpeak (gesture recognition, interpreter-held phone, trie + frequency) and FlexType (touch, VelociTap n-gram decoder) are the nearest. Clarte's numbers must come from its own pilot.
2. How the word boundary is signalled with only eight actionable zones: AC-as-accept (recommended above), a dedicated space key, dwell-timeout, or a glance to the middle band. Each changes the action count per word.
3. Auto-commit of top-1 versus explicit confirmation: FlexType auto-committed, GazeSpeak confirmed per word. With gaze, a wrong auto-commit costs two actions (Other word), a confirm costs one action per word. The break-even depends on the real top-1 rate.
4. Optimal group count if the bottom row could ever hold more than four reliable targets (5 groups: 87.6% unigram top-1; 6: 91.1%), versus two-step selection within a group for rare words.
5. Whether the four group colours in the text line help or distract after learning; GazeSpeak only used length as the cue.
6. How much partner-speech context helps group decoding specifically (as opposed to abbreviation expansion), and the privacy/consent UI for recording a partner.
7. Vertical gaze reliability: if the bottom row is tracked worse than the top row (down-gaze recognition was weakest in GazeSpeak), the G keys may belong on the top row. This is lane 1's measurement to make.
8. Personalisation and OOV handling: dictionary size, adding names from the partner's speech, and the exact "Spell" interaction.
